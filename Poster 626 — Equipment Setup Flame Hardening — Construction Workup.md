# Poster #626 -- Construction Workup
## Equipment Setup -- Flame Hardening

*Alaina -- Plating Posters Inc Poster Designer*
*v1.0 -- 2026-04-26*

Flame hardening equipment is beautifully simple: a fuel gas, an oxygen supply, a torch, and a water spray. No power supply, no vacuum chamber, no atmosphere generator. But within that simplicity, flame adjustment is an art -- the difference between a neutral flame and a carburizing flame matters, and the choice between acetylene and propane affects both the flame temperature and the resulting case. This poster is the equipment setup reference.

| Panel | X | Flame Type | Accent | Description | Use for Flame Hardening? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5" | NEUTRAL | `#27AE60` | Equal oxygen and fuel; sharp inner cone; quiet flame; no excess carbon or oxygen | YES -- PREFERRED. Neutral flame neither adds nor removes carbon from the steel surface. This is the standard flame for hardening. |
| 2 | 8.17" | REDUCING (CARBURIZING) | `#E8A020` | Slight excess fuel; feathery acetylene feather on inner cone; adds carbon to surface | ACCEPTABLE. Slightly reducing flame helps prevent surface oxidation. Some operators prefer a slight carburizing flame for flame hardening. |
| 3 | 15.83" | OXIDIZING | `#E05C5C` | Excess oxygen; shorter, hissing inner cone; pointed; removes carbon from surface | NO -- AVOID. Oxidizing flame decarburizes the steel surface, reducing achievable hardness. Never use for flame hardening. |

### ZONE 5 -- Fuel Gas Comparison + Quench Setup

**Two-column layout (Y: 22.2" to 32.3"):**

**Left -- Fuel Gas Comparison (X: 0.5", W: 11.0"):**

Section label: `ACETYLENE VS. PROPANE` Barlow Condensed ExtraBold 24 pt.

Table:

| Property | Acetylene | Propane |
|---|---|---|
| Flame temperature | 5,600--6,300 F (3,093--3,482 C) | ~4,530 F (~2,499 C) |
| Max working pressure | 15 psig (NEVER exceed) | Up to 250 psig |
| Heating rate | Very fast (highest flame temp) | Moderate |
| Safety risk | HIGH (explosive above 15 psig) | LOWER (narrower flammability range) |
| Cost per BTU | Higher | Lower |
| Soot tendency | Low (clean combustion) | Low-moderate |
| Best application | Thin case; fast heating; small parts | Deep case; large parts; extended heating |
| Availability | Standard (welding supply) | Standard (industrial/welding supply) |

Bottom note: `For most flame hardening, acetylene is preferred because its higher temperature produces faster heating and shallower case. Propane is chosen for deep-case work on large parts where extended flame dwell is needed.` Inter Medium 12 pt `#E8A020`

Content (Inter Regular 14 pt `#F0EDE8`, line height 160%):

```
QUENCH MEDIUM:
  Water (standard -- no polymer needed
  for most flame hardening)

DELIVERY METHOD:
  Spray bar: follows 0.5--2.0 in (13--51 mm)
  behind the flame in progressive hardening

  Immersion: part lowered into water tank
  after spot or spin hardening

  Self-quench: heat sinks into cold core
  mass on very large parts (supplemental
  only -- not reliable as primary quench)

FLOW RATE:
  5--20 gpm depending on part size

WATER TEMPERATURE:
  Ambient (60--80 F / 16--27 C)

SPRAY ALIGNMENT:
  Spray must cover the ENTIRE heated zone.
  Partial coverage = soft spots + distortion.

NO POLYMER NEEDED:
  Unlike induction, flame hardening typically
  uses plain water. The slower heating rate
  and deeper case reduce cracking risk.
```

## Design Notes

The flame type guide is the centerpiece educational content on this poster. Many operators set flames by eye and habit, but understanding WHY a neutral flame is preferred (no decarburization, no carburization) makes them better operators. The acetylene vs. propane comparison answers a practical question: propane is cheaper and safer but produces a cooler flame and slower heating. For shops that flame harden regularly, the fuel choice affects cycle time and case depth.
