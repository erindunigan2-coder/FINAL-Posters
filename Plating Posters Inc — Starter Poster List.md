## Poster Concepts

### 1. Surface Preparation: The Foundation of Every Flawless Finish

**Tagline idea:** "Skip the prep. Ruin the part."

**Core idea:** A vertical flowchart walking through the pre-treatment sequence — cleaning, rinsing, etching, desmutting, activating — with a callout explaining *why* each step matters chemically. Emphasizes that adhesion failures almost always trace back to surface prep, not the plating bath itself.

**Primary audience:** Shop floor operators, new hires, QC inspectors

**Best wall location:** Near the pre-treatment line or employee break room

**Layout concept:** Vertical flowchart with large step icons, color-coded by bath type (clean = blue, acid = orange, rinse = light gray). A bold "failure mode" callout box at the bottom showing what skip-the-prep actually looks like (blistering, peeling, poor adhesion).

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm the correct chemistry sequence for a typical rack zinc pre-treatment line (alkaline clean → rinse → acid activate → rinse) and whether electrolytic cleaning order matters
- Tyler: Validate that "adhesion failures trace to surface prep" holds across barrel zinc, rack nickel, and copper strike operations

**Why this one is compelling:** It's universal. Every plating shop runs pre-treatment regardless of what they're plating. It's also the single most common source of field problems Drew deals with. High relevance, immediate usefulness.

### 2. The Electroplating Process — Step by Step

**Tagline idea:** "Ions in motion. Finish in formation."

**Core idea:** A clean, visually rich diagram of the electroplating cell — power supply, anode, cathode, electrolyte bath — with labeled arrows showing ion movement, electron flow, and where the deposit forms. Includes the simplified Faraday relationship and key variables (current density, temperature, agitation, bath chemistry).

**Primary audience:** New hires, plant visitors, boardroom display

**Best wall location:** Boardroom or training room — this is the "explain our business to a customer" poster

**Layout concept:** Hero diagram (center of poster) showing the electroplating cell in cross-section, rendered in clean industrial illustration style. Surrounding callout boxes address each variable. Clean two-column footer with key terminology defined.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm ion migration direction conventions and whether the "anode dissolves / cathode builds" simplification is accurate for both soluble and insoluble anodes

**Why this one is compelling:** Every shop has customers who don't fully understand what plating is. This is the poster that makes a plating shop look like they know their craft.

---

### 3. Zinc Plating at a Glance: Acid Chloride vs. Alkaline Non-Cyanide

**Tagline idea:** "Same goal. Different chemistry. Know the difference."

**Core idea:** A side-by-side comparison of potassium chloride (acid chloride) zinc and alkaline non-cyanide zinc plating — covering bath composition, operating pH, current density range, deposit characteristics, passivation compatibility, and typical applications. Honest about the tradeoffs of each.

**Primary audience:** Process engineers, QC leads, shop supervisors

**Best wall location:** Lab or quality control area, process control station

**Layout concept:** Two-column comparison layout with a shared header row, alternating row shading for readability. Each column gets a distinct accent color (acid = warm amber, alkaline = cool blue). A "When to choose which" decision box anchors the bottom.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm current density ranges, pH ranges, and deposit hardness/ductility comparisons for modern KCl and alkaline NaCN-free zinc baths
- Tyler: Validate that black chromate compatibility differs between acid and alkaline zinc deposits — this is a common shop floor question

**Why this one is compelling:** Drew's customers include shops running both bath types (Alber's Plating, Delta Specialty Coatings). This poster directly supports technical conversations in the field.

### 4. Reading Your Hull Cell Panel

**Tagline idea:** "The plating bath is trying to tell you something. Here's how to listen."

**Core idea:** A large, annotated Hull cell panel diagram showing the current density gradient (low CD to high CD), and what common deposit defects at each zone indicate — burning at the high end, poor coverage at the low end, pitting, brightness variations, haze bands. Includes a quick parameter-adjustment guide: "If you see X, try Y."

**Primary audience:** Lab technicians, process chemists, QC leads

**Best wall location:** Lab wall, directly above or near the Hull cell station

**Layout concept:** The Hull cell panel takes up the top 60% of the poster as a large, realistic illustration. The bottom 40% is a two-column diagnostic table: left column = "What you see," right column = "What it means / What to do." Color-coded zones on the panel (green = acceptable range, orange = caution, red = problem zone).

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm typical Hull cell current densities (mA range) and the current density values at the high and low ends of a standard 267 mL Hull cell at 2A total current
- Tyler: Validate the defect-to-cause mapping for at least zinc chloride and nickel baths — these are the two Drew's customers run most

**Why this one is compelling:** The Hull cell is the heartbeat of the plating lab. This poster would live on the lab wall and get referenced constantly. High repeat-use value.

### 5. Anode-to-Cathode Ratio: Why It Matters More Than You Think

**Tagline idea:** "Out of ratio. Out of control."

**Core idea:** Explains the anode-to-cathode surface area ratio concept, why it affects bath chemistry (especially for soluble anodes — copper, nickel, zinc), what happens when the ratio drifts too high or too low, and how to estimate it for a given tank and workload. Includes a worked example.

**Primary audience:** Process engineers, shop supervisors, anyone setting up a new tank

**Best wall location:** Near the plating tanks or in the supervisor's office

**Layout concept:** Left panel: illustrated diagram of a plating tank showing anode and cathode arrangement with labeled surface areas. Right panel: explanation of what happens above and below the ideal ratio range, with a worked calculation example. Bold callout box: "Rule of thumb: 1:1 to 2:1 anode:cathode for most soluble anode systems."

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm the ideal A:C ratio ranges for copper sulfate, nickel sulfamate, and zinc chloride baths — and whether alkaline zinc (insoluble anodes) needs a different treatment here

**Why this one is compelling:** This is a fundamental concept that's easy to get wrong and rarely explained visually. Shops that are burning through anodes too fast or watching their bath drift are often violating this without realizing it.

### 6. The Passivation Sequence: From Plated Part to Protected Part

**Tagline idea:** "The zinc is just the start."

**Core idea:** A step-by-step flowchart of the post-plating passivation sequence for zinc — from the plated part through the passivate bath, through the sealer (if applicable), through the drying stage — with key control parameters at each step (pH, time, temperature, drag-out management). Includes trivalent vs. hexavalent passivate comparison callout.

**Primary audience:** Operators, QC inspectors, line supervisors

**Best wall location:** Near the passivation and finishing end of the zinc plating line

**Layout concept:** Horizontal flowchart with large step blocks, icons, and control parameter tags hanging below each step block. A split callout box comparing trivalent (RoHS compliant, becoming standard) vs. hexavalent passivates (legacy, still in use in some shops). Color: blue-silver-gold spectrum suggesting the actual visual progression of the passivate film.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm pH and immersion time ranges for trivalent blue/clear, trivalent yellow, and hexavalent yellow chromate passivates on zinc; include iron contamination limits
- Tyler: Confirm the 150 ppm Fe³⁺ limit already documented in Drew's Quick Reference as the threshold for hex passivate bath contamination — and whether the same limit applies to trivalent baths

**Why this one is compelling:** Directly relevant to Drew's existing customer base (Morrell Plating / BriteGuard NZP, Delta Specialty Coatings). Also connects to A Brite's product line.

### 7. Metallic Contamination — Know Your Thresholds

**Tagline idea:** "A little is normal. Too much is a problem. Know where the line is."

**Core idea:** A reference table and visual guide to common metallic contaminants in plating baths — copper, iron, lead, zinc, chromium — with threshold concentrations for the most common bath types (acid zinc, alkaline zinc, nickel sulfamate, bright acid copper). Includes purification methods (dummy plating, low-current electrolysis, carbon treatment, precipitation).

**Primary audience:** Lab technicians, process chemists

**Best wall location:** Lab — this is a technical reference poster

**Layout concept:** Anchor element is a bold reference table (contaminant × bath type × threshold ppm × symptom × remedy). Surrounding the table: small illustrated panels showing what contamination looks like on a deposit — dark streaks, pitting, roughness, poor adhesion. A "First Response" callout box with the purification decision tree.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Provide contamination thresholds for copper, iron, and lead in acid zinc KCl, alkaline zinc, and nickel sulfamate baths; confirm purification method effectiveness hierarchy

**Why this one is compelling:** This is the poster that makes a lab tech dangerous in the best possible way. Watson already did contamination research for the Acid Zinc Troubleshooting Guide — much of that content can be repurposed here.

### 8. Faraday's Law in the Shop: Calculating Plating Thickness

**Tagline idea:** "The math behind the metal."

**Core idea:** A practical guide to using Faraday's Law to estimate plating thickness from amperage, time, efficiency, and part surface area. Includes the working formula, unit conversions, a step-by-step worked example (zinc on steel, nickel on steel), and a quick-reference table of electrochemical equivalents for common metals.

**Primary audience:** Process engineers, lab techs, QC — anyone who needs to hit a thickness spec

**Best wall location:** Lab or engineering office

**Layout concept:** Formula presented large and bold at the top. Below it, a numbered worked-example walkthrough. Right column: a reference table of key constants (atomic weight, valence, density, electrochemical equivalent) for Zn, Ni, Cu, Cr, Ag, Sn. Footer callout: "Cathode efficiency matters — use measured CE, not 100%."

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm electrochemical equivalent values and typical cathode efficiencies for Zn, Ni, Cu, Cr, Ag, Sn

**Why this one is compelling:** Plating thickness is what the customer is buying. Every shop that holds a thickness spec needs this calculation, and almost nobody has it on the wall where operators can actually use it.

### 9. Anodizing Fundamentals: Type I, II, and III at a Glance

**Tagline idea:** "Same process. Very different results. Know your type."

**Core idea:** A comparison of anodizing Types I (chromic acid), II (sulfuric acid), and III (hard anodize / sulfuric acid, higher current density) — covering electrolyte, operating conditions, typical coating thickness, hardness, porosity, and end-use applications. Includes a brief note on dyeing and sealing.

**Primary audience:** Engineers, QC, plant visitors — shops that run aluminum

**Best wall location:** Anodizing line area or boardroom for aluminum-focused shops

**Layout concept:** Three-column layout (one per type), each with a distinct color band at the top (Type I = olive/gray, Type II = silver/blue, Type III = gunmetal/black). Rows cover: electrolyte, voltage range, thickness range, hardness, applications. Footer: sealing options and their effect on corrosion resistance.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm operating parameters (voltage, temperature, current density, electrolyte concentration) for Types I, II, and III; confirm typical hardness range for Type III (hardcoat) in Rockwell or Vickers

**Why this one is compelling:** Drew's ABB prospect is a 51-station line on aluminum. This poster could be directly relevant to that relationship and others in the aluminum finishing space.

### 10. Electroless Nickel — Process Overview and Bath Control

**Tagline idea:** "No current required. No shortcuts tolerated."

**Core idea:** An overview of the electroless nickel (EN) process — the autocatalytic deposition mechanism, bath components (nickel source, reducing agent, complexants, stabilizers), key control parameters (pH, temperature, nickel concentration, phosphorus content, bath load), and how they interact. Includes a simplified stability diagram.

**Primary audience:** Process engineers, QC, lab techs at EN shops

**Best wall location:** Near the EN tank or in the lab

**Layout concept:** Center: illustrated diagram of the autocatalytic EN mechanism (nickel ion + hypophosphite → nickel deposit + phosphite + hydrogen gas). Surrounding quadrants: bath components, key parameters with ranges, common problems (skip plating, exothermic decomposition, low deposition rate) and their causes. Bold safety callout: bath decomposition hazard.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm the EN deposition reaction mechanism, typical pH range (acid vs. mid-phosphorus vs. high-phosphorus baths), and key stability indicators

**Why this one is compelling:** Qorvo's automatic plating line project includes EN. This poster is directly relevant to Drew's largest active prospect and to any shop in the EN space.

### 11. Current Density Quick Reference Chart

**Tagline idea:** "Right range. Right deposit. Every time."

**Core idea:** A large reference chart showing recommended current density ranges (ASF — amps per square foot) for the most common electroplating processes: acid zinc, alkaline zinc, bright acid copper, cyanide copper strike, nickel sulfamate, hard chrome, decorative chrome, silver, tin, and EN (note: not applicable for EN). Rack and barrel columns where they differ.

**Primary audience:** Operators, line supervisors, setup technicians

**Best wall location:** Near the plating tanks — the most "grab it quick" reference on the floor

**Layout concept:** Horizontal bar chart format — each metal gets a horizontal range bar showing min to max ASF, with an optimal zone highlighted. Rack vs. barrel shown as layered bars. Color-coded by metal family (base metals = blue tones, precious metals = gold tones, chrome = red).

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Provide current density ranges (ASF) for acid zinc KCl, alkaline zinc, bright acid copper, cyanide copper strike, nickel sulfamate, decorative chrome, hard chrome, silver cyanide, and matte tin baths — rack and barrel where applicable

**Why this one is compelling:** Operators reach for this information constantly. It is currently living in binders, vendor sheets, and collective memory. Getting it onto one clean wall chart saves time and reduces errors every single day.

### 12. The pH Control Poster

**Tagline idea:** "pH is not a suggestion."

**Core idea:** A visual guide to pH monitoring and adjustment in plating baths — why pH matters (deposit quality, bath stability, efficiency), how to measure it correctly (meter calibration, electrode care), how to adjust it up and down safely for common bath types, and what out-of-range pH looks like on a deposit.

**Primary audience:** Operators, lab techs — anyone running pH checks on the floor

**Best wall location:** Near the lab bench or bath-side pH meter station

**Layout concept:** pH scale graphic running horizontally across the top half, with colored bands for each major bath type's target range. Below: two-column adjustment guide ("pH too low → add X," "pH too high → add Y") for zinc, nickel, EN, and copper. Safety callout: acid/caustic handling for pH adjusters.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm target pH ranges and corrective chemicals for acid zinc, alkaline zinc, nickel sulfamate, EN (acid and alkaline), and bright acid copper baths

**Why this one is compelling:** pH is the variable operators check most often and adjust most frequently. Getting it wrong is extremely common and extremely consequential. This poster would get daily use.

### 13. Barrel vs. Rack Plating — Choosing the Right Method

**Tagline idea:** "Not every part belongs in a barrel."

**Core idea:** A side-by-side comparison of barrel and rack plating — covering part geometry requirements, size/weight limits, deposit uniformity, throughput, cost per part, and typical applications. Includes a decision guide: "When barrel wins" and "When rack wins."

**Primary audience:** Sales engineers, process engineers, shop supervisors — anyone quoting or setting up a new job

**Best wall location:** Estimating/quoting area, engineering office, or sales meeting room

**Layout concept:** Split layout — left half = barrel plating with illustrated cross-section of barrel and rotating parts, right half = rack plating with illustrated rack and hung parts. Shared comparison table at the bottom. Decision flowchart: part geometry → fragility → volume → choose.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm the generally accepted upper weight/size limits for barrel plating (when does it become impractical?), and whether current density uniformity data comparing barrel vs. rack is documented in industry literature

**Why this one is compelling:** This poster works in a sales context as much as a process context. It's the kind of thing Drew could point to during a customer visit to explain why a given job requires rack plating — or why barrel is the economical choice.

### 14. Safety in the Plating Shop: Chemical Hazard Quick Reference

**Tagline idea:** "Know what you're working with. Every shift, every day."

**Core idea:** A safety-focused quick reference covering the most hazardous chemical families found in plating shops — hexavalent chromium, cyanides, strong acids (hydrochloric, sulfuric, nitric), strong caustics — with hazard symbols, exposure routes, first aid responses, and required PPE. Designed to complement, not replace, SDS documentation.

**Primary audience:** All shop floor personnel, new hires, safety officers

**Best wall location:** Break room, near chemical storage, near the pre-treatment line — anywhere OSHA requires hazard communication postings

**Layout concept:** Grid layout with one large panel per chemical family. Each panel: hazard icon (GHS-compliant), hazard description, exposure route icons (skin, inhalation, ingestion), PPE icons (gloves, goggles, respirator), first aid summary. Bold disclaimer at bottom: "This poster does not replace your SDS. In any emergency, call 911."

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm GHS hazard classifications for hexavalent chromium, sodium cyanide, hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, and sodium hydroxide — especially inhalation thresholds and first aid protocols
- Note: Tyler not required here — this is reference/safety content, not process chemistry

**Why this one is compelling:** Every plating shop is legally required to provide hazard communication. A well-designed poster that makes safety information scannable and memorable serves a real compliance function — and it demonstrates that Plating Posters Inc covers the full spectrum of shop floor needs, not just process optimization.

### 15. The Plating Shop Quality Loop: From Incoming Part to Final Inspection

**Tagline idea:** "Quality isn't the final step. It's every step."

**Core idea:** A circular/cyclical diagram showing the quality control touchpoints across the full plating workflow — incoming part inspection, pre-treatment verification, bath control (Hull cell, titration, pH), in-process visual checks, post-plate thickness measurement, passivation/seal verification, and final inspection against spec. Connects each touchpoint to the quality outcome it protects.

**Primary audience:** QC managers, plant supervisors, ISO/AS9100 auditors, plant visitors

**Best wall location:** Quality control area, ISO/AS9100 documentation wall, boardroom

**Layout concept:** Circular flow diagram (like a quality management system wheel) with each touchpoint as a "station" on the wheel. Each station has an icon, a one-line description, and a "what breaks if you skip this" consequence label. Center of the wheel: "Certified. Every Time." Footer: reference to ISO 9001 and AS9100D where applicable.

**Collaboration flags:**
- Watson: Confirm whether ASTM or AMS specifications govern plating thickness measurement requirements in aerospace contexts (relevant for AS9100D shops like Qorvo prospect)
- Note: This poster has direct relevance to Drew's work with AZZ Galvanizing (quality reporting via ZendoLIMS) and the Qorvo automatic plating line project

**Why this one is compelling:** This is the poster for the boardroom and the quality wall. It speaks the language of auditors, aerospace customers, and quality-conscious buyers. It signals that the shop is running a disciplined operation — and it gives Drew a natural talking point when discussing ZendoLIMS or A Brite's lab support services.
