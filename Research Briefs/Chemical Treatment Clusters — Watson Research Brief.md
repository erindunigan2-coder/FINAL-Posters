# CLUSTER 1: ALKALINE CLEANING (SOAK)

---

## 1.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Alkaline soak cleaning removes organic soils — oils, greases, drawing compounds, fingerprints, buffing compounds, and mill oils — from metal substrates prior to electroplating or further surface treatment. It is the first wet process step on virtually every plating line.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** Steel, stainless steel, copper alloys, zinc die castings, aluminum (with caution — requires low-caustic, silicated formulations to prevent etching)
- **Contaminants removed:** Mineral oils, animal and vegetable fats (saponifiable oils), synthetic lubricants, shop dirt, metal fines, fingerprints, light corrosion products

### Position in Plating Line Sequence
First wet process station. Typical sequence: **Alkaline Soak Clean -> Rinse -> Electroclean -> Rinse -> Acid Activate -> Rinse -> Plate**

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Rack or barrel parts
2. Immerse in heated alkaline soak cleaner
3. Allow soak time per substrate and soil load
4. Withdraw slowly (allow drainage to minimize dragout)
5. Transfer to rinse station
6. Rinse (single or double)
7. Proceed to electrocleaner or acid activation

---

## 1.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards
- **Caustic burns:** NaOH and KOH solutions at operating concentration (4-8 oz/gal / 30-60 g/L) cause severe skin and eye burns on contact
- **Heat hazard:** Operating temperatures of 140-190 deg F (60-88 deg C) increase burn severity
- **Mist/vapor:** Heated alkaline solutions generate alkaline mist, especially with agitation; irritating to respiratory tract
- **Silicate hazard:** Sodium silicate can cause corneal damage — particularly dangerous for eye splash

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | Chemical splash goggles minimum; face shield recommended when making additions |
| Gloves | Heavy-duty rubber or neoprene, chemical-resistant, gauntlet-style (12 in. minimum) |
| Apron | Chemical-resistant rubber or PVC apron |
| Footwear | Chemical-resistant boots or shoe covers; no open-toed shoes |
| Respiratory | Not typically required with adequate ventilation; if mist is present, use half-face respirator with P100 cartridge |

### Emergency Response
- **Skin contact:** Flush immediately with large quantities of water for minimum 15 minutes; remove contaminated clothing
- **Eye contact:** Flush with water or saline for minimum 15 minutes; seek immediate medical attention — alkaline burns worsen over time (liquefactive necrosis)
- **Ingestion:** Do NOT induce vomiting; give water to dilute; immediate medical attention

### Ventilation
- Local exhaust ventilation at tank lip recommended for tanks operating above 140 deg F (60 deg C)
- Minimum 100 FPM capture velocity at tank edge per ACGIH guidelines
- Slot ventilation or push-pull ventilation systems are standard

### OSHA Notes
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.134 — Respiratory Protection (if airborne limits exceeded)
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.151 — Medical services and first aid (eyewash and safety shower within 10 seconds travel time)
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1200 — Hazard Communication (SDS availability)
- NaOH PEL: 2 mg/m3 ceiling (OSHA)

## 1.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Chemical Composition

| Component | Function | Concentration Range |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) or potassium hydroxide (KOH) | Alkaline builder; saponification of fats | 2-6 oz/gal (15-45 g/L) |
| Sodium carbonate (soda ash) | Buffer; maintain alkalinity | 1-4 oz/gal (7.5-30 g/L) |
| Sodium metasilicate | Emulsifier; aluminum-safe corrosion inhibitor | 0.5-3 oz/gal (3.7-22 g/L) |
| Sodium tripolyphosphate or STPP | Dispersant; water softener; complexes Ca/Mg | 0.5-2 oz/gal (3.7-15 g/L) |
| Surfactant/wetting agent | Emulsification; reduces surface tension; aids penetration | 0.1-0.5% by volume (proprietary) |
| Chelator (gluconate, EDTA, or MGDA) | Complexes metal ions (Fe, Ca, Mg) to prevent redeposition | 0-2 oz/gal (0-15 g/L) |

**Total alkalinity (typical):** 4-12 oz/gal (30-90 g/L) as Na2CO3 equivalent

### Temperature
- **Standard operation:** 140-180 deg F (60-82 deg C)
- **Heavy-duty (buffing compounds, heavy oils):** 170-200 deg F (77-93 deg C)
- **Aluminum-safe:** 100-150 deg F (38-66 deg C) — lower temperature + silicate to prevent etch

### Titration / Control Methods
- **Free alkalinity:** Titrate with standard acid (HCl or H2SO4) to phenolphthalein endpoint (pH ~8.3). Report in points (1 point = 1 mL of 1N acid per 10 mL sample)
- **Total alkalinity:** Titrate with standard acid to methyl orange endpoint (pH ~4.3)
- **pH:** Generally 12.0-14.0 (not the primary control metric; alkalinity titration is more informative)
- **Surfactant level:** Rarely titrated in the shop; controlled by replenishment schedule

### Bath Life Indicators — When to Dump
- Excessive soil loading (oil layer on surface that does not emulsify)
- Parts emerging with residual soil after normal soak time
- Alkalinity cannot be maintained without excessive chemical addition
- Foam collapse (surfactant depletion)
- Metal content buildup causing redeposition (zinc die castings dissolving in high-caustic cleaner)
- Typical bath life: 3-6 months with maintenance; longer with oil skimming and filtration

## 1.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Mechanism of Cleaning Action
1. **Saponification:** NaOH reacts with animal/vegetable fats (saponifiable oils) to form water-soluble soaps: Fat + NaOH -> Glycerol + Soap
2. **Emulsification:** Surfactants reduce interfacial tension between oil and water, forming an emulsion that suspends oil droplets in solution
3. **Chelation:** Chelating agents complex dissolved metals (Fe, Ca, Mg) to prevent redeposition as insoluble soaps or oxides
4. **Displacement:** Wetting agents penetrate beneath soil films, lifting them from the substrate surface
5. **Dispersion:** Builders (phosphates, carbonates) disperse suspended soils to prevent re-settling

### Immersion Time
| Substrate / Soil | Time |
|---|---|
| Light oils on steel | 3-5 minutes |
| Heavy drawing compounds | 5-10 minutes |
| Buffing compounds | 8-15 minutes |
| Barrel loads | 10-20 minutes (reduced solution contact due to part nesting) |

### Part Loading Considerations
- **Rack:** Parts must be positioned to allow solution access to all surfaces; avoid nesting or stacking
- **Barrel:** Rotation speed 4-8 RPM; longer immersion times needed due to reduced solution contact; barrel perforations must allow free solution exchange
- **Avoid:** Overloading racks (creates solution depletion zones); trapping air pockets in blind holes

### Common Cleaning Failures and Diagnostics

| Failure | Likely Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Water break on flat surfaces | Insufficient soak time or depleted bath | Extend time; check alkalinity |
| Staining after clean | Dissolved metals redepositing | Check chelator level; dump if metal-loaded |
| Etching (aluminum) | Caustic too high; temperature too high; no silicate inhibitor | Reduce NaOH; add silicate; lower temperature |
| Foam overflow | Excess surfactant dragged in from previous process | Reduce surfactant addition |
| Oil floating on tank | Emulsifier depleted; soil load exceeds capacity | Skim oil; add surfactant; partial dump and recharge |

---

## 1.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse Design
- **Minimum:** Single overflow rinse after soak clean
- **Preferred:** Double counterflow (cascade) rinse — significantly reduces dragout contamination of downstream processes
- **Water type:** City water is acceptable for post-soak-clean rinse (this is not the final pre-plate rinse)
- DI water is NOT required at this stage

### Dragout Reduction
- Withdraw parts slowly from soak cleaner (5-10 second drain time over tank)
- Use drag-out rinse (stagnant first rinse) before flowing rinse to extend cleaner life
- Air knives or drip shields between tanks

### Conductivity Targets
- Post-soak-clean rinse does not have a strict conductivity target because it feeds into electrocleaner (not directly into plating)
- General guideline: rinse water conductivity below 500 microsiemens/cm indicates adequate rinsing
- If feeding directly into acid activation: conductivity below 200 microsiemens/cm preferred to avoid neutralizing acid

---

## 1.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Acid Neutralization After Alkaline Cleaning
- Parts exiting alkaline soak clean carry alkaline dragout
- If proceeding to electroclean (also alkaline), no neutralization needed between the two
- If proceeding directly to acid activation/pickle: the rinse between the two stages serves as the neutralization step — the part surface pH must drop to near-neutral before acid contact to avoid violent neutralization and spotting
- **No separate neutralization tank** is typically used between alkaline clean and acid activate in standard plating lines — the rinse water handles this

### Why This Matters
- Alkaline residues carried into acid baths neutralize the acid, increasing chemical consumption
- Alkaline residues on part surfaces can cause uneven etching in acid activation
- Conversely, carrying too much acid back into an alkaline cleaner raises alkalinity titration variability

## 1.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Water Break Test
**Definition:** After rinsing, water should sheet uniformly across the entire part surface in an unbroken film. If water beads up or breaks into droplets, organic contamination remains.

- **Pass:** Continuous water film for minimum 30 seconds with no break
- **Fail:** Any area where water beads or pulls away from the surface
- **Standard reference:** ASTM F22 — Standard Test Method for Hydrophobic Surface Films by the Water-Break Test

### Visual Inspection Criteria
- No visible oil sheen, fingerprints, or residual soil
- No etching, pitting, or discoloration (especially on aluminum and zinc die cast)
- No water spots from drying between process steps

### Clean Part Handling
- **Wear clean, lint-free gloves** (cotton or nitrile) when handling cleaned parts
- **Never touch cleaned parts with bare hands** — skin oils cause immediate water-break failure
- **Minimize dwell time** between cleaning and subsequent process steps — cleaned parts begin to oxidize immediately
- **Dry storage:** If parts cannot proceed immediately, keep submerged in clean rinse water or dry in a clean, oil-free environment within 30 minutes

### Common Surface Defects from Improper Cleaning
| Defect | Cause |
|---|---|
| Blistering after plating | Residual oil under deposit |
| Skip plating (bare spots) | Organic film preventing adhesion |
| Rough deposit | Embedded particles not removed during cleaning |
| Staining | Redeposition of dissolved metals from spent cleaner |
| Etching | Over-aggressive cleaner (too hot, too caustic, too long) |

---

## 1.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### Waste Classification
- Spent alkaline cleaner is typically a **D002 hazardous waste** (corrosivity, pH >= 12.5) under EPA 40 CFR 261.22
- If metals (Cr, Cd, Pb, etc.) are dissolved in the cleaner, additional D-characteristic codes may apply (D007 for Cr, D006 for Cd, etc.)
- Alkaline cleaners are NOT F-listed (F-listed wastes are specific to certain manufacturing processes)

### Discharge
- Must be pH-adjusted to 6.0-9.0 before discharge to POTW (publicly owned treatment works) per 40 CFR 403 pretreatment standards
- Oil and grease limits per local POTW permit (typically 100 mg/L or less)

# CLUSTER 2: ELECTROCLEANING

---

## 2.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Electrocleaning uses electrolysis to generate gas at the part surface (hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode), creating a mechanical scrubbing action that removes tenacious soils, smut, and residual contamination that soak cleaning alone cannot remove. It is the final cleaning step before acid activation and plating.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** Steel, stainless steel, copper alloys, nickel alloys, zinc die castings (with caution — limited current density)
- **Contaminants removed:** Residual oils missed by soak clean, surface smut, light oxide films, polishing compound residues, embedded particles

### Position in Plating Line Sequence
Follows soak clean and rinse. Precedes acid activation. Typical: **Soak Clean -> Rinse -> Electroclean -> Rinse -> Acid Activate -> Rinse -> Plate**

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Transfer racked parts from rinse to electrocleaner tank
2. Connect parts to electrical bus bar
3. Apply current (cathodic, anodic, or periodic reverse per specification)
4. Maintain immersion for specified time
5. Remove current and disconnect
6. Withdraw slowly from tank
7. Transfer to rinse

---

## 2.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards
- **Caustic burns:** Identical to soak cleaner — NaOH-based solutions at 4-8 oz/gal
- **Electrical hazard:** Rectifier output typically 6-12 V DC; current can be substantial (hundreds of amperes); risk of shock and arc flash at bus bars
- **Hydrogen gas generation:** Cathodic cleaning generates hydrogen at the workpiece; **hydrogen is flammable and explosive** at 4-75% concentration in air (LEL 4%, UEL 75%)
- **Oxygen generation:** Anodic cleaning generates oxygen; supports combustion; less hazardous than hydrogen but contributes to explosion risk if hydrogen is also present

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | Chemical splash goggles + face shield when working near energized bus bars |
| Gloves | Rubber or neoprene chemical-resistant gloves; **insulated gloves** when handling bus bars |
| Apron | Chemical-resistant rubber or PVC |
| Footwear | Chemical-resistant, non-conductive boots |
| Respiratory | Not typically required with adequate ventilation |

### Emergency Response
- Same as alkaline soak cleaning for chemical contact
- **Electrical:** De-energize rectifier before retrieving dropped parts or making bus bar adjustments; lockout/tagout per OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147
- **Hydrogen accumulation:** Ensure continuous ventilation; never seal or cover electrocleaner tanks; post "No Smoking" signs

### Ventilation
- **Critical** for electrocleaning — hydrogen gas must be dispersed
- Local exhaust ventilation at tank lip: minimum 100-150 FPM capture velocity
- Tank covers should NOT be sealed — allow gas to vent
- Explosion-proof electrical equipment in the immediate vicinity per NEC Article 500

### OSHA Notes
- 29 CFR 1910.147 — Lockout/Tagout (electrical)
- 29 CFR 1910.303-308 — Electrical safety
- 29 CFR 1910.151 — Eyewash/shower
- Hydrogen gas: OSHA does not set a specific PEL; regulated as a simple asphyxiant and explosion hazard

---

## 2.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Chemical Composition

| Component | Function | Concentration Range |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) | Alkaline builder; conductivity | 3-8 oz/gal (22-60 g/L) |
| Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) | Buffer; maintains alkalinity | 2-6 oz/gal (15-45 g/L) |
| Sodium phosphate (trisodium phosphate, TSP) | Emulsifier; dispersant | 1-4 oz/gal (7.5-30 g/L) |
| Surfactant/wetting agent | Soil emulsification; foam control | 0.05-0.3% by volume |
| Chelator (gluconate, EDTA) | Metal ion complexation | 0-1 oz/gal (0-7.5 g/L) |

**Note:** Electrocleaner formulations are simpler than soak cleaners — the electrical action does much of the heavy lifting, so fewer chemical agents are needed.

### Temperature
- **Standard:** 140-180 deg F (60-82 deg C)
- **Typical target:** 150-160 deg F (66-71 deg C)
- Higher temperature increases cleaning efficiency and solution conductivity

### Titration / Control Methods
- **Free alkalinity:** Titrate 10 mL sample with N/10 HCl or H2SO4 to phenolphthalein endpoint; report in points
- **Total alkalinity:** Titrate to methyl orange endpoint
- **Typical control:** Free alkalinity 5-10 points; total alkalinity 8-15 points (varies by proprietary formula)
- **Chloride contamination check:** If etching is suspected, titrate for chlorides using silver nitrate / potassium chromate (Mohr method). Per Drew's field notes: **10 g/L chlorides is too high** and causes substrate corrosion that leads to salt spray failure after plating.

### Bath Life Indicators
- Persistent soil on parts after normal cycle
- Excessive foaming or no foaming (surfactant imbalance)
- High metal content in solution (dissolved substrate metals)
- Alkalinity dropping rapidly between maintenance additions
- Typical bath life: 6-12 months with proper maintenance

---

## 2.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Mechanism of Cleaning Action
The primary mechanism is **gas evolution scrubbing:**
- At the **cathode** (workpiece cathodic): 2H2O + 2e- -> H2(gas) + 2OH-
- At the **anode** (workpiece anodic): 2OH- -> H2O + 1/2 O2(gas) + 2e-
- Gas bubbles nucleate at the metal surface, physically dislodging soil, smut, and oxide films
- Anodic cleaning generates approximately half the gas volume as cathodic (per mole of electrons), but oxygen bubbles are larger and provide effective scrubbing

### Current Density and Polarity

| Parameter | Cathodic (Direct) | Anodic (Reverse) |
|---|---|---|
| Current density | 20-50 ASF (2.2-5.4 A/dm2) | 20-75 ASF (2.2-8.1 A/dm2) |
| Voltage (typical) | 6-9 V | 6-12 V |
| Gas evolved | Hydrogen (H2) | Oxygen (O2) |
| Risk | Hydrogen embrittlement; metal smut deposition from solution impurities | Slight metal dissolution (etching); surface oxidation |

### When to Use Each Polarity

| Mode | Application |
|---|---|
| **Anodic (Reverse)** | Preferred for most applications; no HE risk; slight etching promotes adhesion; standard for steel, copper alloys |
| **Cathodic (Direct)** | Used when anodic dissolution/etching is unacceptable (e.g., highly polished surfaces, tin-plated parts); generates more gas volume; **carries hydrogen embrittlement risk** |
| **Periodic Reverse (PR)** | Alternates cathodic and anodic; best of both; used for difficult soils; typical cycle: 10 sec cathodic / 10 sec anodic |

### Immersion Time
- **Standard:** 30-120 seconds (0.5-2 minutes)
- **Heavy soil:** Up to 3 minutes
- **Zinc die castings:** 15-30 seconds maximum (alkaline dissolution risk)
- **Note from Drew's field data:** Long immersion times in electrocleaner can cause etching, especially with low caustic or chloride contamination

### Common Cleaning Failures

| Failure | Likely Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Etching / pitting | Chloride contamination; low alkalinity; excessive voltage | Titrate chlorides (>10 g/L = problem); check alkalinity; reduce voltage |
| Smut on surface | Cathodic cleaning depositing metal impurities from solution | Switch to anodic; dump if heavily contaminated |
| Parts still oily | Depleted surfactant; insufficient current; low temperature | Check surfactant; verify current flow; check heater |
| Hydrogen embrittlement | Excessive cathodic cleaning time on high-strength steel | Limit cathodic time; use anodic; bake per ASTM B850 |
| No gas evolution | Open circuit; bad contact; blown fuse | Check electrical connections; verify rectifier output |

---

## 2.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse Design
- **Minimum:** Single overflow rinse
- **Preferred:** Double counterflow cascade rinse
- **Critical:** This rinse must remove all alkaline residue before acid activation — alkaline carryover neutralizes acid, causing poor activation and adhesion failure

### Water Type
- City water acceptable at this stage
- DI water not required

### Dragout Reduction
- Slow withdrawal (5-10 sec drain)
- Drag-out rinse (stagnant first rinse tank)
- Air blow-off

### Conductivity Targets
- Pre-acid-activation rinse: below 200 microsiemens/cm preferred
- Below 50 microsiemens/cm ideal for critical applications (aerospace, electronics)

---

## 2.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Relationship to Acid Activation
- Electrocleaning is itself a secondary treatment (after soak clean)
- After electrocleaning and rinsing, parts proceed directly to **acid activation** (acid dip/pickle)
- No neutralization step is needed — the rinse provides the transition from alkaline to acid

### Hydrogen Embrittlement Considerations
- If cathodic electrocleaning was used on high-strength steel (Rc >= 39 / tensile >= 180 ksi), a hydrogen embrittlement relief bake may be required:
  - **Bake temperature:** 375 +/- 25 deg F (190 +/- 14 deg C) per ASTM B850
  - **Bake time:** Minimum 4 hours (8-24 hours for ultra-high-strength steels)
  - **Timing:** Within 4 hours of the hydrogen-generating process
- This bake is typically done after plating, not after electrocleaning alone, but the cleaning step contributes to total hydrogen exposure

---

## 2.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Water Break Test
- Same as soak clean: continuous water film, no beading, minimum 30 seconds
- ASTM F22 reference
- **The water break test after electrocleaning is the most critical pass/fail gate on the plating line** — if the part fails here, every subsequent process will fail

### Visual Criteria
- No smut, staining, or etching
- Bright, clean metallic surface
- No gas pitting (from excessive current density)

### Handling
- Wear clean gloves at all times after electrocleaning
- Transfer parts to acid activation within 5 minutes — oxide formation begins immediately
- Never allow cleaned parts to air-dry between electroclean and acid activation

---

## 2.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### Waste Classification
- Same as soak cleaner: D002 (corrosivity) if pH >= 12.5
- Metal-loaded electrocleaner may carry additional D-characteristic codes
- **Hydrogen gas:** Not a waste issue but an explosion hazard — NFPA 70 (NEC) and NFPA 497 apply

### Electrical
- Rectifiers must be maintained per manufacturer specifications
- Bus bars: regular inspection for corrosion, loose connections, arcing

# CLUSTER 3: ACID PICKLING (STEEL / CARBON STEEL)

---

## 3.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Acid pickling removes mill scale, rust, heat treat scale, and oxide films from carbon steel and low-alloy steel surfaces, providing a chemically clean, active surface for plating adhesion.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** Carbon steel, low-alloy steel, cast iron
- **Contaminants removed:** Mill scale (FeO, Fe2O3, Fe3O4), rust (hydrated iron oxides), heat treat scale, weld scale, light corrosion products

### Position in Plating Line Sequence
Follows electrocleaning and rinse. Equivalent to the "acid activation" step. Typical: **Electroclean -> Rinse -> Acid Pickle -> Rinse -> Plate**

For heavy-scale work, pickling may be a standalone operation preceding the entire plating line.

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Transfer parts from rinse to pickle tank
2. Immerse in acid solution
3. Allow pickle time (monitor for complete scale removal)
4. Agitate if needed (air agitation, not mechanical)
5. Withdraw parts
6. Transfer immediately to rinse
7. Rinse thoroughly (double rinse preferred)

---

## 3.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards
- **Acid burns:** HCl (15-50% solutions) and H2SO4 (5-25% solutions) cause severe chemical burns
- **Fume generation:** HCl pickle generates hydrochloric acid fumes (highly irritating, corrosive); fuming increases significantly at elevated temperature
- **Hydrogen gas:** Acid dissolving iron generates hydrogen: Fe + 2HCl -> FeCl2 + H2(gas); explosion risk
- **Exothermic reactions:** Adding concentrated H2SO4 to water generates heat (always add acid to water, never water to acid)
- **Splashing:** Parts entering pickle with water can cause splashing from hydrogen evolution on scale

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | Chemical splash goggles **and** face shield (acid splash is high-velocity from hydrogen evolution) |
| Gloves | Heavy-duty butyl rubber or neoprene, 14 in. minimum gauntlet; PVC acceptable for HCl |
| Apron | Full chemical splash apron (PVC or rubber) |
| Body | Chemical splash suit for tank maintenance or additions |
| Footwear | Chemical-resistant boots |
| Respiratory | Half-face or full-face respirator with acid gas cartridge (organic vapor/acid gas combination); required for HCl pickle without adequate ventilation |

### Emergency Response
- **Skin contact:** Flush immediately with large volumes of water for 15-20 minutes; remove contaminated clothing
- **Eye contact:** Flush with water or saline for minimum 15-20 minutes; immediate medical attention
- **HCl inhalation:** Move to fresh air immediately; administer oxygen if available; seek medical attention
- **H2SO4 splash:** Same as HCl skin protocol; concentrated H2SO4 causes dehydration burns — flush copiously

### Ventilation
- **HCl pickle tanks require ventilation** — HCl fumes at all temperatures; worse above 100 deg F
- Local exhaust at tank lip: 150-200 FPM minimum capture velocity for HCl
- H2SO4 pickle (operated hot at 130-160 deg F): ventilation required for acid mist
- Fume scrubber recommended (packed tower with NaOH solution or water) for HCl fumes
- ACGIH TLV: HCl = 2 ppm ceiling; H2SO4 = 0.2 mg/m3 (TWA)
- OSHA PEL: HCl = 5 ppm ceiling; H2SO4 = 1 mg/m3 (TWA)

### OSHA Notes
- 29 CFR 1910.1000 — Air contaminants (PELs for HCl and H2SO4)
- 29 CFR 1910.134 — Respiratory protection
- 29 CFR 1910.151 — Emergency eyewash/shower within 10 seconds
- 29 CFR 1910.1200 — Hazard Communication

---

## 3.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Chemical Composition — Hydrochloric Acid Pickle

| Component | Concentration |
|---|---|
| Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 20 deg Be / ~31.45%) | 15-50% by volume of concentrated acid in water (actual HCl content: 5-16% w/w) |
| **Typical working strength** | 10-20% HCl by weight (100-200 g/L HCl) |
| Pickle inhibitor | 0.1-0.5% by volume (proprietary) |
| Iron content (dissolved) | Monitor; dump when Fe exceeds 8-10% w/w (80-100 g/L Fe) |

### Chemical Composition — Sulfuric Acid Pickle

| Component | Concentration |
|---|---|
| Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 66 deg Be / 93-98%) | 5-25% by volume of concentrated acid in water |
| **Typical working strength** | 10-15% H2SO4 by weight (100-150 g/L) |
| Pickle inhibitor | 0.1-0.5% by volume (proprietary) |
| Iron content (dissolved) | Monitor; dump when Fe exceeds 6-8% w/w (60-80 g/L Fe) — sulfuric pickle saturates at lower Fe than HCl |

### Temperature

| Acid | Operating Temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HCl | Ambient to 110 deg F (ambient to 43 deg C) | Do NOT heat HCl pickle — fuming becomes extreme above 110 deg F; most HCl pickling is done at room temperature |
| H2SO4 | 130-160 deg F (54-71 deg C) | H2SO4 pickle is sluggish at room temperature; heating is required for practical pickling rates |

### HCl vs. H2SO4 — Selection Criteria

| Factor | HCl | H2SO4 |
|---|---|---|
| Pickling speed | Faster at room temperature | Requires heat to achieve comparable speed |
| Scale removal | Excellent on all scale types | Better on heavy mill scale |
| Fumes | Heavy fuming (requires ventilation) | Low fuming at operating temperature |
| Hydrogen embrittlement | Lower risk (faster pickle = less H exposure) | Higher risk (longer immersion + heat) |
| Cost | Higher acid cost; lower energy cost | Lower acid cost; higher energy cost (heating) |
| Spent liquor | Easier to regenerate (spray roasting) | Harder to regenerate |

### Titration Method — Free Acid
- **Pipette** 5 or 10 mL of pickle solution into an Erlenmeyer flask
- **Dilute** with 50-100 mL DI water
- **Add indicator:** Methyl orange (or bromophenol blue for better visibility in iron-rich solutions)
- **Titrate** with standard NaOH (0.5N or 1.0N) to endpoint (yellow to orange for methyl orange)
- **Calculate** free acid in g/L or percent
- **Iron content:** Titrate separately using permanganate (KMnO4) method or EDTA complexometric titration; alternatively, measure specific gravity — higher SG at constant acid strength indicates higher dissolved iron

### Bath Life Indicators
- Pickling rate slows dramatically (visual: hydrogen evolution decreases)
- Iron content exceeds 8-10% for HCl, 6-8% for H2SO4
- Free acid drops below minimum despite additions
- Parts emerging with residual scale after extended immersion
- Color change: fresh HCl pickle is clear/pale yellow; spent pickle is dark brown/green-black

---

## 3.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Mechanism of Acid Dissolution
- **Primary reaction:** Acid dissolves iron oxide scale:
  - FeO + 2HCl -> FeCl2 + H2O
  - Fe2O3 + 6HCl -> 2FeCl3 + 3H2O
  - Fe3O4 + 8HCl -> FeCl2 + 2FeCl3 + 4H2O
- **Secondary reaction:** Acid attacks base metal:
  - Fe + 2HCl -> FeCl2 + H2(gas)
- **Inhibitors** suppress the secondary reaction (base metal attack) while allowing scale dissolution to continue

### Immersion Time

| Condition | HCl Pickle | H2SO4 Pickle |
|---|---|---|
| Light rust / light scale | 1-5 minutes | 5-15 minutes |
| Medium mill scale | 5-15 minutes | 15-30 minutes |
| Heavy heat treat scale | 15-30 minutes | 30-60 minutes |

**Caution:** Over-pickling causes pitting, hydrogen embrittlement, and excessive base metal loss.

### Part Loading Considerations
- **Rack:** Standard; ensure acid contacts all surfaces
- **Barrel:** HCl barrel pickle at room temperature is common; rotation provides agitation
- **Coils/Strip:** Continuous pickle lines use HCl at ambient with squeegee rolls
- **Avoid:** Stacking parts (traps acid in crevices); dissimilar metals in same bath (galvanic acceleration)

### Common Pickling Failures

| Failure | Likely Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Under-pickling (residual scale) | Acid depleted; iron too high; time too short | Check free acid; check iron content |
| Over-pickling (pitting, rough surface) | Too long; too strong; no inhibitor | Reduce time; check acid strength; add inhibitor |
| Hydrogen embrittlement | Excessive time; no inhibitor; high-strength steel | Use inhibited acid; minimize time; bake per ASTM B850 |
| Flash rusting after pickle | Inadequate rinsing; parts air-drying before plate | Rinse immediately; keep parts wet |
| Smut (dark residue) | Carbon smut from high-carbon steel; alloying element residues | Normal for some alloys; remove in subsequent process |

---

## 3.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse Design
- **Double counterflow cascade rinse** strongly recommended after pickle
- First rinse captures bulk acid dragout; second rinse achieves final cleanliness
- Rinse water pH should recover to near-neutral (pH 5-7)

### Water Type
- City water acceptable
- DI water preferred for final rinse before plating (especially for decorative applications)

### Dragout Reduction
- Slow withdrawal with 10-15 second drain over pickle tank
- Drag-in rinse (dedicated first rinse) to capture concentrated dragout
- Fog rinse (mist spray) above pickle tank

### Conductivity Targets
- Pre-plating rinse after pickle: below 100 microsiemens/cm preferred
- Below 50 microsiemens/cm for critical applications
- Acid carryover into plating baths causes pH depression, chloride contamination (from HCl pickle), and sulfate contamination (from H2SO4 pickle)

---

## 3.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Pickle Inhibitors — What They Do and Why

**Purpose:** Inhibitors are organic compounds that adsorb onto clean metal surfaces, forming a protective film that reduces acid attack on the base metal while allowing acid to continue dissolving oxide scale.

**How they work:**
- Oxide scale has a different surface energy than clean iron — inhibitors preferentially adsorb to clean metal
- They reduce hydrogen evolution (and thus hydrogen absorption/embrittlement)
- They reduce metal loss (important for dimensional tolerance work)

**Common inhibitor chemistries:**
- Thiourea and derivatives (traditional; effective but can cause sulfur contamination in some plating processes)
- Quaternary ammonium compounds
- Propargyl alcohol and acetylenic compounds
- Proprietary blends (most commercial inhibitors are multi-component)

**Concentration:** Typically 0.1-0.5% by volume of the working pickle solution

**Limitations:**
- Inhibitors are consumed over time and must be replenished
- Effectiveness decreases as temperature increases (especially above 160 deg F)
- Some inhibitors can contaminate subsequent plating baths if not rinsed thoroughly

### Hydrogen Embrittlement Mitigation
- Use inhibited acid whenever pickling high-strength steel (Rc >= 39)
- Minimize immersion time
- Bake per ASTM B850 / AMS 2759/9 within 4 hours: 375 +/- 25 deg F for 4-24 hours depending on tensile strength
- Document pickle time for traceability (AS9100D, aerospace)

---

## 3.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Post-Pickle Inspection
- Surface should be uniformly bright (for low-carbon steel) or uniformly matte gray (for high-carbon steel)
- No residual scale, rust, or oxide
- No pitting from over-pickling
- Carbon smut on high-carbon steel is expected and acceptable — removed in subsequent plating processes

### Water Break Test
- Applicable but less definitive after pickle (acid-clean surfaces are hydrophilic by nature)
- More useful after alkaline cleaning

### Handling
- **Do not allow pickled parts to dry** — flash rusting begins within minutes
- Transfer immediately to rinse, then to plating
- Wear clean nitrile or latex gloves
- If parts must wait, keep submerged in dilute acid or inhibited rinse water

### Common Defects

| Defect | Cause |
|---|---|
| Flash rust (orange/brown film) | Parts dried between pickle and plate; inadequate rinsing |
| Pitting | Over-pickling; acid too strong; no inhibitor |
| Hydrogen blisters | Hydrogen absorbed during pickle; visible as raised spots after plating |
| Dark smut | Carbon residue from high-carbon steel; alloying element oxides |

---

## 3.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### EPA Waste Classification — Spent Pickle Liquor

**This is one of the most heavily regulated waste streams in metal finishing.**

| Waste Type | EPA Classification | Regulation |
|---|---|---|
| Spent HCl pickle liquor (steel) | **D002** (corrosivity, pH < 2.0) | 40 CFR 261.22 |
| Spent H2SO4 pickle liquor (steel) | **D002** (corrosivity) | 40 CFR 261.22 |
| If chromium present (from stainless or chromium-plated parts) | **D007** (Cr toxicity, TCLP >= 5.0 mg/L) | 40 CFR 261.24 |
| If lead present | **D008** (Pb toxicity, TCLP >= 5.0 mg/L) | 40 CFR 261.24 |

**F006 Note:** F006 (wastewater treatment sludges from electroplating operations) applies to the sludge generated when pickle liquor is neutralized for disposal — NOT to the liquid pickle itself. However, if the pickle liquor is treated on-site and generates a sludge, that sludge is **F006 listed hazardous waste** regardless of its constituent concentrations.

### Air Emissions
- HCl fumes: regulated under Clean Air Act; may require packed-tower scrubber
- H2SO4 mist: regulated as a hazardous air pollutant (HAP) under Clean Air Act Section 112

### Hydrogen Gas
- Not a regulated waste but an **explosion hazard**
- NFPA 30 and NEC Article 500 apply
- Ventilation must keep hydrogen below 25% of LEL (1% in air) at all times

# CLUSTER 4: ACID PICKLING (STAINLESS STEEL)

---

## 4.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Acid pickling of stainless steel removes heat tint (weld discoloration), thermal oxide scale from heat treatment or welding, and surface contamination (embedded iron, free iron) that would otherwise compromise the passive chromium oxide film and cause corrosion.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** Austenitic stainless (304, 316, 321, 347), ferritic stainless (430, 409), martensitic stainless (410, 420), duplex stainless, precipitation-hardened stainless (17-4 PH)
- **Contaminants removed:** Thermal oxide/heat tint (from welding, annealing, brazing), embedded iron (from fabrication contact with carbon steel tools), chromium-depleted surface layer

### Position in Sequence
Can be standalone (fabrication shop descaling after welding) or part of a plating preparation line. For plating: **Alkaline Clean -> Rinse -> Stainless Pickle -> Rinse -> [Passivation or Strike Plate]**

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Verify alloy type (determines acid mixture)
2. Immerse in appropriate acid pickle solution
3. Allow pickle time per scale severity and alloy
4. Monitor for complete scale removal (visual)
5. Withdraw parts
6. Rinse thoroughly (minimum double rinse)
7. Passivate or proceed to next process step

---

## 4.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards
- **Nitric acid (HNO3):** Strong oxidizing acid; reacts violently with organic materials; causes severe burns; generates toxic nitrogen oxide (NOx) fumes (brown/orange gas)
- **Hydrofluoric acid (HF):** Extremely dangerous; penetrates skin rapidly; causes deep tissue destruction and potentially fatal hypocalcemia (calcium depletion) even from small skin exposure areas; **delayed onset** — burns may not be felt for hours
- **Mixed acid (HNO3 + HF):** Standard stainless pickle; combines hazards of both acids
- **Chromic acid:** Sometimes used for specific alloys; hexavalent chromium is a known carcinogen

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | **Full face shield over chemical splash goggles** — non-negotiable for HF-containing solutions |
| Gloves | **Butyl rubber or thick neoprene** (NOT latex; NOT nitrile for HF — HF penetrates thin nitrile); double-glove recommended; minimum 14 in. gauntlet |
| Apron/Suit | Full chemical splash suit (PVC or butyl rubber) for tank maintenance; heavy PVC apron minimum for routine work |
| Footwear | Chemical-resistant boots with splash guards |
| Respiratory | Full-face respirator with acid gas/P100 combination cartridge minimum; supplied-air respirator for enclosed areas or tank entry |

### Emergency Response
- **HF skin contact — MEDICAL EMERGENCY:** Flush with water for 5 minutes, then apply **calcium gluconate gel (2.5%)** to exposed area; massage continuously; seek immediate emergency medical treatment. HF burns can be fatal even from small exposures.
- **HF eye contact:** Flush with water for 15 minutes minimum; seek emergency ophthalmological care
- **NOx inhalation (from HNO3):** Move to fresh air; NOx exposure may cause delayed pulmonary edema (6-24 hours after exposure); all exposures require medical monitoring
- **Every shop using HF must stock calcium gluconate gel and train all personnel in its use**

### Ventilation
- **Mandatory local exhaust ventilation** — NOx fumes from nitric acid are immediately dangerous
- Minimum 150-200 FPM capture velocity at tank edge
- Fume scrubber (packed tower with NaOH or water) strongly recommended
- ACGIH TLV: HF = 0.5 ppm (TWA), 2 ppm (ceiling); HNO3 = 2 ppm (TWA), 4 ppm (STEL)
- OSHA PEL: HF = 3 ppm (TWA); HNO3 = 2 ppm (TWA)

### OSHA Notes
- 29 CFR 1910.1000 — PELs for HF, HNO3, NOx, Cr(VI) if chromic acid used
- 29 CFR 1910.1026 — Chromium (VI) standard (if chromic acid pickle used)
- 29 CFR 1910.134 — Respiratory protection
- 29 CFR 1910.151 — Emergency eyewash/shower (within 10 seconds)

---

## 4.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Chemical Composition — Nitric-Hydrofluoric Acid Pickle (Standard)

| Component | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nitric acid (HNO3, 42 deg Be / ~67-70%) | 10-25% by volume (70-175 g/L HNO3) | Oxidizer; dissolves chromium oxides; passivates |
| Hydrofluoric acid (HF, 48-52%) | 1-8% by volume (5-40 g/L HF) | Dissolves siliceous scale; attacks oxide films that HNO3 alone cannot |
| Water | Balance | Always add acid to water |

**Typical working bath:** 15-20% HNO3 + 2-5% HF by volume (for austenitic stainless)

### Chemical Composition — Nitric Acid Pickle (HF-Free)

| Component | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nitric acid | 15-30% by volume (105-210 g/L) | Used alone for lighter scale; also for passivation |

Used when HF hazards are unacceptable or for light oxide removal. Slower on heavy scale.

### Chemical Composition — Alternative: Sulfuric-Hydrofluoric Pickle

| Component | Concentration |
|---|---|
| Sulfuric acid (H2SO4) | 8-12% by volume |
| Hydrofluoric acid (HF) | 1-3% by volume |

Used for heavy descaling of ferritic and martensitic grades. Operated hot (140-160 deg F / 60-71 deg C).

### Temperature

| Bath Type | Temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HNO3 + HF | 70-130 deg F (21-54 deg C) | Room temperature to mildly heated; excessive heat accelerates HF fuming |
| HNO3 only | 70-140 deg F (21-60 deg C) | Can tolerate higher temperature since no HF fumes |
| H2SO4 + HF | 140-160 deg F (60-71 deg C) | Requires heat for effectiveness |

### Titration / Control Methods
- **Free nitric acid:** Titrate with NaOH to phenolphthalein endpoint; report as % HNO3 or g/L
- **Free hydrofluoric acid:** Titrate with NaOH after adding saturated potassium nitrate (KNO3) to suppress HNO3 interference; endpoint with phenolphthalein. Alternatively, titrate total acid with NaOH, subtract nitric acid portion
- **Dissolved metals (Fe, Cr, Ni):** Monitor by specific gravity increase, color change (green = chromium/nickel buildup), or analytical methods (AA spectroscopy)

### Bath Life Indicators
- Dissolved metal content above 40-50 g/L total metals
- Solution turns deep green/blue-green (chromium and nickel ions)
- Pickling rate drops significantly
- Free acid depleted despite additions
- Typical bath life: 2-4 weeks for production; longer for intermittent use

---

## 4.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Mechanism
- **HNO3:** Oxidizing acid; dissolves chromium-depleted layer and iron contamination; simultaneously passivates the stainless surface by promoting formation of a new chromium oxide film
- **HF:** Dissolves siliceous and refractory oxide scale that nitric alone cannot penetrate; attacks silicon-bearing inclusions
- **Combined action:** HNO3 oxidizes iron and chromium; HF attacks the scale matrix; together they lift the entire scale layer

### Immersion Time

| Condition | HNO3 + HF | HNO3 Only |
|---|---|---|
| Light heat tint | 5-15 minutes | 15-30 minutes |
| Medium weld scale | 15-30 minutes | 30-60 minutes |
| Heavy anneal scale | 30-60 minutes | Not practical (too slow) |

### Part Loading
- **Rack preferred** — critical to avoid crevice attack in overlapping areas
- **Basket immersion** for small parts
- **Barrel NOT recommended** for stainless pickle — crevice corrosion risk between parts
- Avoid rubber bands, wire ties, or anything that creates crevice geometry

### Common Failures

| Failure | Likely Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Over-pickling (grain boundary attack, rough surface) | Too long; HF too high; temperature too high | Reduce time; check acid concentrations |
| Under-pickling (residual scale) | Acid depleted; metals too high; time too short | Check free acid; replace solution |
| Intergranular attack (IGA) | Sensitized material (304 not stabilized); HNO3 too high relative to HF | Metallurgical issue; adjust acid ratio |
| Preferential attack on welds | Heat-affected zone metallurgy; acid imbalance | May require pre-grinding of weld scale |
| Orange peel texture | Excessive HF; too long | Reduce HF; reduce time |

---

## 4.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse Design
- **Triple rinse minimum** recommended for stainless pickle (HF residue is extremely dangerous)
- First rinse: dedicated drag-out capture (stagnant or slow flow)
- Second and third: flowing counterflow cascade
- Final rinse water must test negative for fluoride (test with fluoride-specific ion electrode or colorimetric kit)

### Water Type
- City water for bulk rinsing
- DI water for final rinse (especially before passivation)

### Dragout Reduction
- Slow withdrawal with extended drain time (15 sec minimum)
- Parts should be completely free of acid before handling
- Drag-out rinse captures concentrated HNO3/HF for return to pickle tank or waste treatment

### Conductivity Targets
- Below 50 microsiemens/cm for pre-passivation rinse
- Fluoride residue is more critical than conductivity — verify by fluoride ion testing

---

## 4.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Passivation After Pickle
Stainless steel requires **passivation** after pickling to restore and optimize the protective chromium oxide film.

**Standard passivation per ASTM A967 / AMS 2700:**

| Method | Solution | Temperature | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitric acid passivation (Method 1) | 20-50% HNO3 by volume | 70-140 deg F (21-60 deg C) | 20-30 minutes |
| Nitric acid + sodium dichromate (Method 2) | 20-50% HNO3 + 2-6 oz/gal Na2Cr2O7 | 120-140 deg F (49-60 deg C) | 20-30 minutes |
| Citric acid passivation (Method 5) | 4-10% citric acid by weight | 70-160 deg F (21-71 deg C) | 4-20 minutes |

**Citric acid passivation** is increasingly favored as an environmentally friendly alternative to nitric acid, per ASTM A967 Method 5.

### Passivation Verification
- **Copper sulfate test (ASTM A967 Practice E):** Apply copper sulfate solution; if copper deposits (pink/copper color within 6 seconds), free iron is present = fail
- **High-humidity test (ASTM A967 Practice C):** Expose to high humidity (>97% RH) at 100-115 deg F for 24 hours; any rust = fail
- **Salt spray (ASTM A967 Practice B):** 5% NaCl fog per ASTM B117 for 2-24 hours; any rust = fail

---

## 4.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Post-Pickle / Post-Passivation Inspection
- Surface should be uniformly bright and metallic (austenitic grades) or uniformly matte (ferritic grades)
- No residual heat tint, scale, or embedded iron
- No preferential weld attack, grain boundary grooving, or orange peel texture
- Verification per ASTM A380 (cleaning, descaling, passivation of stainless steel)

### Handling
- Wear clean nitrile or cotton gloves — fingerprints on passivated stainless can cause localized corrosion
- Avoid contact with carbon steel tools, fixtures, or work surfaces (iron transfer causes tea staining)
- Store in clean, dry environment; wrap in VCI (vapor corrosion inhibitor) paper for long-term storage
- If plating follows: proceed to strike plating within 30 minutes

### Common Defects

| Defect | Cause |
|---|---|
| Tea staining (rust spots) | Free iron contamination from handling, tooling, or inadequate pickling |
| Grain boundary attack | Over-pickling sensitized material |
| Pitting | HF too concentrated; crevice under fixture |
| Haze / dull surface | Insufficient rinsing; acid residue |

---

## 4.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### Waste Classification
- Spent HNO3/HF pickle liquor: **D002** (corrosivity) plus potentially **D007** (Cr), **D009** (Ni — not currently D-listed but may be present in sludge)
- HF waste is a **D002** hazardous waste and may require specific handling as an acute hazard
- **Chromic acid** pickle waste: **D007** (chromium) plus may be regulated as hexavalent chromium — OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1026

### Air Emissions
- NOx fumes from HNO3: regulated HAP
- HF fumes: regulated HAP; OSHA PEL 3 ppm TWA
- Cr(VI) if chromic acid used: OSHA PEL 5 micrograms/m3 (action level 2.5 micrograms/m3)

### Special Fluoride Waste Considerations
- Fluoride-bearing rinse water must be treated to below POTW limits (typically 10-20 mg/L fluoride) before discharge
- Treatment: precipitation with calcium chloride as calcium fluoride (CaF2)

# CLUSTER 5: DESCALING / HEAVY OXIDE REMOVAL

---

## 5.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Descaling removes thick, tenacious oxide scale from metal surfaces — the type of scale that normal acid pickling cannot efficiently remove. This includes heavy mill scale, furnace scale from heat treatment (quenching, annealing, brazing), forge scale, laser cutting scale, and weld scale on alloy steels.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** Carbon steel, alloy steel, stainless steel, titanium, Inconel/nickel alloys, tool steels
- **Contaminants removed:** Mill scale (multi-layer FeO/Fe3O4/Fe2O3), forge scale, heat treat scale, laser/plasma cut oxide, welding scale, heavy corrosion layers

### Position in Sequence
Descaling is typically a **pre-pickle** operation for heavily scaled parts. Sequence: **Descale -> Rinse -> Acid Pickle (conventional) -> Rinse -> [proceed to plating or fabrication]**

For plating shops receiving parts with heavy scale, descaling may be done off-line (batch processing) before parts enter the plating line.

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Assess scale type and thickness (determines method selection)
2. Pre-clean if heavy oil/grease present (alkaline soak or solvent)
3. Immerse in descaling bath or apply mechanical descaling
4. Monitor for scale removal (visual and tactile)
5. Rinse thoroughly
6. Inspect; repeat if necessary
7. Proceed to conventional acid pickle or next process

---

## 5.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards (by Method)

| Method | Primary Hazards |
|---|---|
| Molten salt descaling (oxidizing salt bath) | **Extreme burn hazard** — molten NaOH/NaNO3 at 700-900 deg F (371-482 deg C); exothermic water contact; caustic splash |
| Alkaline permanganate | Potassium permanganate is a strong oxidizer; causes severe skin staining and burns; NaOH burns |
| Acid descaling (H2SO4 + HF, HCl + phosphoric, etc.) | Same as acid pickle hazards — acid burns, HF danger, fumes, hydrogen gas |
| Mechanical descaling (abrasive blasting) | Silicosis (silica sand — banned in many jurisdictions); respiratory hazard from all abrasive dusts; noise (>85 dBA) |

### Required PPE
- **Molten salt:** Full-body aluminized heat-resistant suit, face shield with heat protection, insulated gloves rated for 900+ deg F, leather boots; **absolutely no moisture contact with molten bath**
- **Alkaline permanganate:** Chemical splash goggles, face shield, neoprene gloves, chemical apron
- **Acid descaling:** Same as Cluster 3/4 acid pickle PPE
- **Abrasive blasting:** Blast hood with supplied air, hearing protection (earplugs + earmuffs for >100 dBA), leather gloves, full-body blast suit

### Emergency Response
- **Molten salt splash:** Do NOT apply water to molten salt on skin (steam explosion); smother with dry cloth; seek immediate burn treatment
- **Acid burns:** Flush with water per acid pickle protocols
- **Permanganate skin staining:** Sodium bisulfite solution (1-3%) decolorizes permanganate

### OSHA Notes
- Abrasive blasting: 29 CFR 1910.94 — Ventilation (abrasive blasting)
- Noise: 29 CFR 1910.95 — Occupational noise exposure (PEL: 90 dBA TWA 8-hour)
- Silica: 29 CFR 1910.1053 — Respirable crystalline silica (PEL: 50 micrograms/m3)

---

## 5.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Method 1: Alkaline Permanganate Descaling

| Component | Concentration | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) | 10-15 oz/gal (75-112 g/L) | Alkaline environment; scale conditioning |
| Potassium permanganate (KMnO4) | 3-6 oz/gal (22-45 g/L) | Oxidizer; converts chromium oxides to soluble chromates; conditions scale for acid removal |

- **Temperature:** 180-210 deg F (82-99 deg C) — boiling or near-boiling
- **Immersion time:** 15-60 minutes (depending on scale thickness)
- **Followed by:** Acid pickle (HNO3/HF for stainless; H2SO4 or HCl for carbon steel) to remove conditioned scale

### Method 2: Molten Salt Descaling (Oxidizing Salt Bath)

| Component | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) base + sodium nitrate (NaNO3) oxidizer | 700-900 deg F (371-482 deg C) |

- **Immersion time:** 5-20 minutes
- **Mechanism:** Molten caustic + oxidizer dissolves and conditions even the most tenacious scale
- **Used for:** Inconel, titanium, heavily scaled tool steels, investment casting scale
- **Followed by:** Water quench (careful — violent boiling) then acid pickle

### Method 3: Acid Descaling (Chemical Only)

| Solution | Concentration | Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| H2SO4 + HF | 10-15% H2SO4 + 2-5% HF | 140-170 deg F (60-77 deg C) |
| Ammonium bifluoride | 1-2 oz/gal (7.5-15 g/L) in acid or alkaline carrier | Per Drew's field notes: used for pickle/descale of welds with proprietary products |
| Phosphoric acid + additives | 10-25% H3PO4 | 140-180 deg F (60-82 deg C); slower, less aggressive |

### Method 4: Mechanical Descaling

| Method | Media | Pressure/Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Abrasive blasting (grit blast) | Aluminum oxide, steel grit, glass bead, garnet | 40-100 psi nozzle pressure |
| Wheel blasting (shot blast) | Steel shot or cut wire | Wheel speed 2000-3500 RPM |
| Tumbling / vibratory | Ceramic or steel media | Time-dependent; 1-4 hours |

### Control Methods
- **Alkaline permanganate:** Titrate NaOH (free alkalinity); titrate KMnO4 by permanganate color intensity or by titration with sodium oxalate/ferrous ammonium sulfate
- **Acid descaling:** Same as Cluster 3/4 titration methods
- **Molten salt:** Maintain by additions based on throughput; periodic analysis for sodium carbonate buildup (contaminant from CO2 absorption)

---

## 5.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Scale Types and Recommended Methods

| Scale Type | Recommended Descaling Method |
|---|---|
| Light mill scale on carbon steel | HCl or H2SO4 pickle (no dedicated descale needed) |
| Heavy mill scale on carbon steel | H2SO4 pickle at 150 deg F or abrasive blast + pickle |
| Weld/heat tint on stainless | HNO3 + HF pickle (Cluster 4) |
| Heavy anneal scale on stainless | Alkaline permanganate -> HNO3/HF pickle (two-step) |
| Forge scale on alloy steel | Abrasive blast -> acid pickle |
| Inconel / nickel alloy scale | Molten salt or alkaline permanganate -> HNO3/HF pickle |
| Titanium scale | Molten salt -> HNO3/HF pickle |
| Investment casting scale (ceramic shell residue) | Molten salt or alkaline permanganate |

### Common Failures

| Failure | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Incomplete descaling | Scale too heavy for method chosen; depleted bath | Increase severity; use multi-step approach |
| Substrate damage | Over-aggressive treatment; wrong acid for alloy | Review alloy compatibility; reduce time/concentration |
| Intergranular attack on stainless | Sensitized grain boundaries attacked preferentially | Metallurgical issue — adjust pickle or use mechanical methods |
| Embedded abrasive (from blasting) | Media too aggressive; wrong angle; wrong media | Switch to glass bead or aluminum oxide; reduce pressure |

---

## 5.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse Design
- After chemical descaling: **double or triple counterflow rinse** (same as acid pickle)
- After molten salt: **water quench tank** (first rinse is a quench — violent boiling possible) followed by flowing rinse
- After alkaline permanganate: dedicated rinse before acid pickle (prevent permanganate carryover into acid — violent reaction possible)

### Dragout
- Chemical descaling baths are concentrated; dragout is significant
- Extended drain times (15-20 seconds) essential
- Drag-out rinse tanks recommended

---

## 5.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Post-Descale Acid Pickle
- Descaling **conditions** the scale but may not fully remove it
- A conventional acid pickle (Cluster 3 or 4 depending on substrate) is required after most descaling operations to achieve a bright, clean surface
- Sequence: Descale -> Rinse -> Pickle -> Rinse

### Neutralization
- After descaling + pickling, parts carry residual acid
- Standard rinse + neutralization protocols apply (see Cluster 8)

---

## 5.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Post-Descale Inspection
- All scale removed (visual and tactile — run gloved finger across surface)
- No substrate damage (no pitting, IGA, or dimensional loss beyond tolerance)
- Surface profile within specification (especially after abrasive blasting — measure with surface profile gauge; typical 1-3 mil / 25-75 micrometers for coating adhesion)

### Handling
- Same as acid pickle handling — keep parts wet; do not allow air-drying
- Document descaling parameters (method, time, temperature) for quality traceability

---

## 5.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### Waste Classification
- Spent alkaline permanganate: **D002** (corrosivity) if pH >= 12.5; may also carry **D007** (Cr) if stainless scale dissolved
- Spent acid descaling: Same as Clusters 3/4
- Molten salt: regenerated on-site; spent salt is typically **D002** (corrosivity)
- Abrasive blasting waste: generally non-hazardous unless substrate contains hazardous metals (lead paint, cadmium plating being removed, chromium); test via TCLP

### Air Emissions
- Abrasive blasting: dust collector required (baghouse or cartridge); respirable dust PEL per OSHA
- Chemical descaling: ventilation per acid/alkaline type (see Clusters 1, 3, 4)

# CLUSTER 6: SOLVENT CLEANING / DEGREASING

---

## 6.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Solvent cleaning removes organic soils — heavy greases, waxes, tars, adhesives, and oils — that are not readily emulsified by alkaline cleaners. Solvents dissolve these contaminants directly through dissolution rather than emulsification. Often used as a pre-clean before alkaline cleaning.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** All metals (solvent cleaning is substrate-neutral — solvents do not attack metals)
- **Contaminants removed:** Heavy mineral oils, waxes, greases, tar, adhesive residues, silicone contamination, some polymer films

### Position in Sequence
**Before** alkaline cleaning (if heavy organic soil is present). Typical: **Solvent Clean -> Alkaline Soak Clean -> Rinse -> Electroclean -> Rinse -> Acid Activate -> Rinse -> Plate**

For light soils, solvent cleaning may be omitted entirely.

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Assess soil type and quantity
2. Select solvent and method (cold immersion, vapor degreasing, spray)
3. Immerse, spray, or expose parts to solvent vapor
4. Allow adequate contact time
5. Withdraw from solvent
6. Allow solvent to evaporate (or proceed to alkaline clean)
7. Transfer to next process step

---

## 6.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards

| Solvent Category | Examples | Primary Hazards |
|---|---|---|
| **Chlorinated (halogenated)** | Trichloroethylene (TCE), perchloroethylene (PERC), methylene chloride | Liver/kidney toxicity; CNS depression; TCE is a known carcinogen (IARC Group 1); PERC is a probable carcinogen |
| **Non-halogenated** | Mineral spirits, naphtha, toluene, xylene, acetone, IPA | Flammable (flash points vary: acetone 0 deg F, mineral spirits 105-145 deg F); CNS effects; respiratory irritation |
| **Modified alcohols / nPB** | n-propyl bromide (nPB / 1-bromopropane) | Neurotoxicity; reproductive toxicity; OSHA proposed PEL 0.1 ppm |
| **Newer solvents** | Trans-1,2-dichloroethylene (trans-DCE), methylsiloxane (D5) | Lower toxicity profiles; still require ventilation |

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | Chemical splash goggles (liquid); safety glasses (vapor only) |
| Gloves | Solvent-resistant gloves specific to the solvent in use — **butyl rubber for chlorinated solvents; nitrile for most non-halogenated** (check SDS compatibility chart) |
| Respiratory | Organic vapor cartridge respirator minimum; supplied-air for enclosed spaces or high concentrations |
| Body | No special clothing for routine vapor degreasing; full chemical suit for tank cleaning or spill response |

### Emergency Response
- **Inhalation:** Move to fresh air immediately; administer oxygen if needed; monitor for delayed pulmonary effects
- **Skin contact:** Remove contaminated clothing; wash with soap and water (NOT solvent — use soap)
- **Ingestion:** Do NOT induce vomiting (aspiration risk for hydrocarbons); immediate medical attention
- **Fire (non-halogenated solvents):** CO2 or dry chemical extinguisher; do NOT use water on solvent fires (spreads)

### Ventilation
- **Chlorinated solvents:** Enclosed vapor degreasing systems with freeboard chillers to capture vapors; NESHAP 40 CFR Part 63 Subpart T compliance required for halogenated solvent degreasing
- **Non-halogenated flammable solvents:** Explosion-proof ventilation; LEL monitoring
- OSHA PELs (selected):
  - TCE: 100 ppm (TWA) — note: ACGIH TLV is 10 ppm; many states adopt the lower limit
  - PERC: 100 ppm (TWA); 200 ppm ceiling
  - Mineral spirits: 500 ppm (TWA) per OSHA; ACGIH TLV = 100 ppm
  - Acetone: 1000 ppm (TWA)
  - nPB: No established OSHA PEL as of 2025; ACGIH TLV = 0.1 ppm (proposed)

---

## 6.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Cold Solvent Immersion
- Solvent used as-received; no dilution
- Maintained by periodic replacement when soil loading exceeds capacity
- **Monitoring:** Visual clarity; if solvent is opaque or heavily discolored, replace
- Solvent recycling: distillation (on-site or off-site) recovers clean solvent from waste

### Vapor Degreasing

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Solvent | TCE, PERC, trans-DCE, nPB, or other vapor degreasing grade |
| Boiling point (TCE) | 189 deg F (87 deg C) |
| Boiling point (PERC) | 250 deg F (121 deg C) |
| Freeboard ratio | Minimum 1.0 (freeboard height / tank width); 1.5 preferred |
| Vapor zone temperature | At solvent boiling point |
| Primary/secondary coils | Maintain vapor line below tank lip; condense escaping vapor |

**Mechanism:** Solvent boils in sump, rises as vapor; cold parts are lowered into vapor zone; vapor condenses on part surface, dissolving soil; clean condensate drips back to sump, carrying dissolved soil.

### Control Methods
- **Solvent purity:** Monitor by specific gravity, boiling point range, or acid acceptance test
- **Stabilizer level:** Chlorinated solvents require acid inhibitors/stabilizers to prevent decomposition; test per solvent manufacturer's procedure
- **Moisture:** Water contamination causes acid formation in chlorinated solvents; monitor and use water separators

### Bath Life Indicators
- Solvent discoloration (dark color = soil loading)
- Boiling point shift (indicates contamination or stabilizer depletion)
- Acid acceptance test failure (chlorinated solvents — indicates decomposition)
- Solvent odor change

---

## 6.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Mechanism
**Dissolution:** Organic solvents dissolve organic contaminants by "like dissolves like." The solvent surrounds and solvates the oil/grease molecules, carrying them into solution. No chemical reaction occurs — this is purely a physical dissolution process.

### Methods

| Method | Description | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Cold immersion | Parts soaked in solvent at room temperature | Moderate — good for light to medium soil |
| Vapor degreasing | Parts exposed to pure solvent vapor | Excellent — fresh condensing solvent is always clean; self-purifying |
| Spray / wipe | Manual application with cloth or spray | Spot cleaning; uneven; labor-intensive |
| Ultrasonic + solvent | Ultrasonic agitation in solvent bath | Superior — combines dissolution with cavitation (see Cluster 7) |

### Contact Time
- **Cold immersion:** 3-15 minutes depending on soil
- **Vapor degreasing:** 1-5 minutes (parts are clean when they reach vapor temperature and condensation stops)
- **Spray/wipe:** Until visually clean

### Part Loading
- Parts must not trap solvent in blind holes or cups (dragout concern and fire/health hazard)
- Fixturing should allow free drainage
- Barrel processing in solvent is unusual — rack or basket preferred

### Common Failures

| Failure | Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Residual soil after solvent clean | Wrong solvent for soil type (polar vs. non-polar mismatch); solvent saturated | Test different solvent; replace bath |
| White residue on parts | Moisture contamination; solvent decomposition products | Check for water; test acid acceptance |
| Solvent attack on non-metallic components | Seals, gaskets, paint attacked by solvent | Verify compatibility of all materials of construction |
| Spotting/staining | Solvent evaporating unevenly; leaving dissolved soil as residue | Use vapor degreasing (self-rinsing); improve drainage |

---

## 6.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse After Solvent Cleaning
- **Water rinse is NOT used directly after solvent cleaning** — solvents and water do not mix
- Parts should **air dry or drain** to allow solvent evaporation before entering aqueous processes
- If proceeding to alkaline soak clean: the alkaline bath itself serves as the next "rinse" — surfactants in the cleaner emulsify any residual solvent film
- In vapor degreasing, the process is self-rinsing (pure condensing solvent is the final "rinse")

### Dragout
- Solvent dragout evaporates (unlike aqueous dragout)
- Environmental concern: solvent vapors must be captured (fume hood, enclosure)
- Minimize dragout of dissolved contaminants by using multi-stage solvent systems (dirty sump -> clean sump -> vapor)

---

## 6.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Transition to Aqueous Processes
- After solvent cleaning, parts typically proceed to alkaline soak cleaning
- The alkaline cleaner removes any residual solvent film and water-soluble contaminants that solvent alone does not address
- Solvent cleaning + alkaline cleaning is a "belt and suspenders" approach for heavily soiled parts

### When Solvent Cleaning is the Final Clean
- Some non-plating applications (electronics, optics, aerospace assembly) use solvent cleaning as the final process
- In these cases, solvent purity is critical — use high-grade solvent and multi-stage rinsing (e.g., cascading solvent immersion)

---

## 6.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Water Break Test
- Not directly applicable after solvent cleaning (parts may not be wetted with water at this stage)
- If parts proceed to aqueous cleaning, the water break test is performed after the aqueous step

### Visual Inspection
- No visible oil, grease, or residue
- No solvent residue (white haze, staining)
- UV fluorescence inspection: some oils fluoresce under UV/blacklight — absence of fluorescence indicates clean surface

### Handling
- Wear clean, lint-free gloves (nitrile preferred — cotton absorbs solvent)
- Handle in well-ventilated area (residual solvent vapor)
- Do not stack or nest parts (traps solvent vapor and contaminants)

---

## 6.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### EPA Waste Classification — Solvent Waste

**This is the most heavily regulated waste category for cleaning operations.**

| Waste Stream | EPA Classification |
|---|---|
| Spent halogenated solvents (TCE, PERC, methylene chloride, chloroform, 1,1,1-TCA) | **F001** — listed hazardous waste (used for degreasing) |
| Spent halogenated solvents (same chemicals, other uses) | **F002** — listed hazardous waste |
| Spent non-halogenated solvents (xylene, acetone, ethyl acetate, ethyl benzene, ethyl ether, methyl isobutyl ketone, n-butyl alcohol, cyclohexanone, methanol) | **F003** — listed hazardous waste (ignitable only) |
| Spent non-halogenated solvents (toluene, methyl ethyl ketone, carbon disulfide, isobutanol, pyridine, cresols/cresylic acid, nitrobenzene) | **F005** — listed hazardous waste (toxic and ignitable) |

**Key point:** F-listed wastes are hazardous regardless of concentration — the "listing" applies to the waste stream, not the analytical result. This is the "mixture rule" and "derived-from rule" under RCRA.

### NESHAP Compliance
- 40 CFR Part 63 Subpart T — National Emission Standards for Halogenated Solvent Cleaning
- Requires enclosed equipment, freeboard controls, idling emission limits
- Applies to all halogenated solvent cleaning machines

### Air Emissions
- VOC emissions from non-halogenated solvents: regulated under Clean Air Act; may require emission controls (carbon adsorption, thermal oxidation)
- HAP emissions from halogenated solvents: NESHAP Subpart T

### Fire Code
- Non-halogenated solvents: NFPA 30 (Flammable and Combustible Liquids Code)
- Storage quantity limits per fire code occupancy classification
- Explosion-proof electrical equipment required in solvent storage and use areas

# CLUSTER 7: ULTRASONIC CLEANING

---

## 7.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Ultrasonic cleaning uses high-frequency sound waves to create cavitation bubbles in a liquid medium. When these bubbles implode at the part surface, they produce intense localized energy that dislodges contaminants from surfaces, blind holes, threads, and complex geometries that immersion cleaning alone cannot reach.

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** All metals, ceramics, glass, plastics (with appropriate frequency/power selection)
- **Contaminants removed:** Buffing/polishing compounds, lapping paste, machining coolant residue, flux residues, carbon deposits, biological films, light oxide, embedded particles
- **Especially effective for:** Complex geometries, blind holes, internal passages, threaded parts, springs, medical instruments, electronic assemblies

### Position in Sequence
Typically replaces or supplements soak cleaning. Can be used at multiple points in a plating line. Common: **Ultrasonic Clean -> Rinse -> Electroclean -> Rinse -> Acid Activate -> Rinse -> Plate**

### 7-Step Process Sequence
1. Select cleaning solution (alkaline, neutral, acidic, or solvent-based per substrate and soil)
2. Set ultrasonic frequency and power level
3. Heat solution to operating temperature (if required)
4. Immerse parts in ultrasonic bath (positioned properly relative to transducers)
5. Run ultrasonic cycle for specified time
6. Remove parts
7. Rinse and proceed to next step

---

## 7.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards
- Dependent on the cleaning solution used (alkaline, acid, or solvent — see respective clusters)
- **Ultrasonic-specific hazard:** Aerosol generation — ultrasonic agitation creates fine mist/aerosol of the cleaning solution; any chemical hazard is amplified by inhalation route
- **Noise hazard:** Ultrasonic frequencies (20-170 kHz) are above human hearing, but subharmonic noise at audible frequencies can reach 80-100 dBA near the tank

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | Chemical splash goggles (aerosol generation) |
| Hearing protection | Earplugs or earmuffs if operating near the tank with the lid open; especially at lower frequencies (20-25 kHz) which generate more audible noise |
| Gloves | Per cleaning solution type |
| Respiratory | If cleaning solution generates hazardous aerosol, use appropriate cartridge respirator |

### Emergency Response
- Per cleaning solution type (see respective cluster)
- **Do NOT put hands in an operating ultrasonic tank** — cavitation can cause tissue damage

### Ventilation
- Tank lid should be closed during operation when possible (reduces aerosol and noise)
- Local exhaust ventilation recommended, especially for solvent-based ultrasonic cleaning

---

## 7.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Cleaning Solutions
Ultrasonic cleaning can use any aqueous or solvent-based cleaning solution. The most common:

| Solution Type | Application | Concentration |
|---|---|---|
| Alkaline aqueous | General metals cleaning; oils, greases | 2-8 oz/gal (15-60 g/L) per alkaline cleaner formulation |
| Neutral/mildly alkaline (pH 7-10) | Sensitive substrates (aluminum, electronics, medical) | Per manufacturer; typically 2-5% by volume |
| Acidic | Oxide removal concurrent with cleaning | 5-15% acid (HCl, citric, phosphoric) |
| Solvent-based | Heavy organic soils; electronics defluxing | As-received; specialty solvents for ultrasonic use |

### Ultrasonic Parameters

| Parameter | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | 20-170 kHz | Lower frequency = larger cavitation bubbles = more aggressive cleaning; higher frequency = smaller bubbles = gentler, better for fine features |
| **Common frequencies** | 25 kHz (general industrial); 40 kHz (standard precision); 68-170 kHz (fine/delicate parts, electronics) | |
| **Power density** | 10-100 watts per gallon (2.6-26 W/L) of tank volume | Higher power = more aggressive; typical industrial: 25-50 W/gal |
| **Temperature** | 100-170 deg F (38-77 deg C) for aqueous solutions | Temperature affects cavitation; optimal varies by solution; too hot reduces cavitation efficiency (degassing) |
| **Optimal cavitation temperature** | 140-160 deg F (60-71 deg C) for most aqueous solutions | Above this, dissolved gas comes out of solution and cushions cavitation |

### Frequency Selection Guide

| Frequency Range | Bubble Size | Cleaning Action | Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20-25 kHz | Large (~130 micrometers) | Aggressive; can damage soft metals | Heavy industrial degreasing, steel, cast iron |
| 40 kHz | Medium (~70 micrometers) | Standard; good balance of power and safety | General-purpose metal cleaning, plating pre-treatment |
| 68-80 kHz | Small (~40 micrometers) | Gentle; reaches fine features | Precision parts, aluminum, thin-walled |
| 100-170 kHz | Very small (~15-25 micrometers) | Very gentle; micro-cleaning | Electronics, semiconductor, medical, optics |

### Control Methods
- Solution concentration: titrate or pH-test per solution type
- Ultrasonic intensity: measure with cavitation meter, aluminum foil erosion test (standardized exposure test), or glass slide cleaning test
- Solution cleanliness: visual clarity; filter and skim as needed
- Replace solution when cleaning effectiveness degrades despite correct parameters

### Bath Life Indicators
- Visible soil accumulation in solution
- Parts not cleaning in normal cycle time
- Foaming that dampens cavitation
- Solution pH drift outside operating range
- Typically replaced more frequently than soak cleaners due to smaller tank volumes

---

## 7.4 — Cleaning / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Mechanism of Cavitation Cleaning
1. **Ultrasonic transducers** (piezoelectric or magnetostrictive) convert electrical energy to mechanical vibrations
2. Vibrations propagate through the liquid as alternating high-pressure (compression) and low-pressure (rarefaction) waves
3. During rarefaction, microscopic vacuum cavities (bubbles) form in the liquid
4. During compression, these bubbles implode violently — temperatures at the implosion site reach approximately 5,000 deg C (9,000 deg F) and pressures reach ~500 atm for microseconds
5. The implosion creates micro-jets of liquid directed at the surface, physically blasting contaminants loose
6. This occurs millions of times per second across the entire wetted surface, including blind holes and complex geometries

### Immersion Time

| Soil / Application | Time |
|---|---|
| Light oils on machined parts | 2-5 minutes |
| Buffing/polishing compounds | 5-10 minutes |
| Flux residues on electronic assemblies | 3-8 minutes |
| Carbon deposits | 10-20 minutes |
| Medical instrument reprocessing | 5-15 minutes (per manufacturer IFU) |

### Part Positioning
- Parts must be **suspended in the ultrasonic field**, not resting on the tank bottom (dampens vibration)
- Use baskets or fixtures made of open mesh (stainless steel wire mesh or perforated sheet)
- Avoid dense packing — parts must be surrounded by liquid for cavitation to occur on all surfaces
- **Shadow effect:** Parts directly behind large objects receive less ultrasonic energy; position critical surfaces facing the transducers

### Common Cleaning Failures

| Failure | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Parts not clean | Wrong frequency for soil type; insufficient power; parts shielded | Adjust frequency; increase power; reposition parts |
| Surface erosion/damage | Frequency too low for substrate; excessive power; too long | Increase frequency; reduce power/time |
| Cavitation dead zones | Standing wave patterns; improper tank fill level | Adjust liquid level; add sweep frequency feature |
| Excessive foaming | Surfactant concentration too high; contamination | Reduce surfactant; use low-foaming formulation |
| No cleaning in blind holes | Air trapped in holes | Rotate parts; use vacuum degassing; orient holes downward |

---

## 7.5 — Rinse System Poster Data

### Rinse Design
- Standard aqueous rinse (same as soak cleaning — see Cluster 1)
- **Ultrasonic rinse** tanks are available and recommended for critical applications — DI water with ultrasonic agitation for final particulate removal
- Double counterflow preferred

### Special Considerations
- Ultrasonic cleaning can loosen contaminants that remain suspended; thorough rinsing is critical to prevent redeposition
- Filtration of rinse water recommended for particulate-sensitive applications (electronics, medical)

---

## 7.6 — Secondary Treatment Poster Data

### Relationship to Other Cleaning Steps
- Ultrasonic cleaning typically replaces or supplements soak cleaning; it does not replace electrocleaning (which provides electrochemical activation)
- After ultrasonic clean: proceed to electroclean or acid activation depending on process sequence
- For non-plating applications (medical, electronics), ultrasonic cleaning may be the final process

### Degassing
- Ultrasonic tanks should be **degassed** before use — run the ultrasonics for 5-10 minutes with fresh solution to drive dissolved gas out
- Dissolved gas cushions cavitation and reduces cleaning effectiveness
- Degassing is also achieved by heating the solution

---

## 7.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Water Break Test
- Applicable after ultrasonic cleaning with aqueous solutions (same as Cluster 1)

### Visual / Instrumental Inspection
- Visual: no visible soil, particles, or residue
- **Particulate count:** For critical applications, use liquid particle counter on final rinse water to verify cleanliness (semiconductor, medical)
- **UV fluorescence:** Check for residual organic contamination under blacklight
- **Gravimetric:** Weigh parts before and after cleaning to quantify soil removal (laboratory method)

### Handling
- Same clean-glove handling protocols as Cluster 1
- Parts cleaned ultrasonically for precision applications may require clean-room handling protocols

---

## 7.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### Waste
- Spent aqueous ultrasonic cleaning solutions: same as alkaline cleaner waste (D002 if pH >= 12.5)
- Spent solvent-based ultrasonic solutions: F001/F002/F003/F005 per solvent type (see Cluster 6)
- Contaminated solution containing metals: characterize per TCLP

### Noise
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.95: noise exposure above 85 dBA requires hearing conservation program; above 90 dBA TWA requires engineering controls
- Ultrasonic tank subharmonic noise can exceed 85 dBA with lid open

### Equipment
- Ultrasonic generators: standard electrical safety per OSHA 29 CFR 1910.303-308
- No special hazardous area classification required (unless solvent-based — then NEC Article 500 applies)

# CLUSTER 8: NEUTRALIZATION & RINSE SYSTEMS

---

## 8.1 — Process Flow Poster Data

### Purpose
Neutralization and rinsing are the **connective tissue** of every plating line. Rinses remove dragout from the previous process step; neutralization tanks adjust surface pH when transitioning between alkaline and acid processes. Improperly designed rinse and neutralization systems are the #1 cause of plating defects attributed to "contamination."

### Substrates and Contaminants
- **Substrates:** All substrates on the plating line
- **Contaminants managed:** Alkaline cleaner residue, acid residue, dissolved metals, surfactants, particulates, process chemical dragout

### Position in Sequence
**Between every process step on the plating line.** A typical line has 4-8 rinse stations.

### 7-Step Rinse System Design Sequence
1. Identify the two adjacent process steps (what comes before and after the rinse)
2. Determine chemical compatibility requirements (acid to alkaline transition = neutralization needed)
3. Select rinse type (single, double counterflow, spray, drag-out)
4. Specify water source (city, DI, mixed)
5. Set flow rate and conductivity target
6. Install conductivity monitoring (if automated)
7. Implement dragout reduction measures

---

## 8.2 — Safety & PPE Poster Data

### Chemical Hazards
- Rinse waters are generally **low hazard** (dilute solutions)
- **Neutralization tanks** contain dilute acid (1-5% H2SO4 or HCl) or dilute alkali (1-5% NaOH) — mild irritant to skin; eye splash is still serious
- **Cross-contamination hazard:** Dragging hexavalent chromium or cyanide into rinse water creates a toxic waste stream (Cr(VI) + acid = hexavalent chromium mist; cyanide + acid = HCN gas — lethal)

### Required PPE
| PPE Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Eye protection | Safety glasses minimum; goggles when making neutralization tank additions |
| Gloves | Nitrile or rubber; standard chemical-resistant |
| Apron | Standard rubber or PVC |

### Emergency Response
- Standard for dilute acid/alkali contact
- **Critical awareness:** If cyanide is used anywhere on the plating line, acid and cyanide rinse waters MUST be segregated — **mixing acid and cyanide generates hydrogen cyanide gas (HCN), which is lethal at 300 ppm**

### Ventilation
- Rinse tanks generally do not require dedicated ventilation
- Neutralization tanks with acid: mild ventilation recommended
- **Exception:** Rinse tanks adjacent to chrome plating or chromic acid processes must have ventilation (Cr(VI) mist)

---

## 8.3 — Bath Preparation & Control Poster Data

### Rinse Water Quality

| Rinse Type | Water Source | Typical Conductivity |
|---|---|---|
| First rinse (drag-out) | City water or recirculated | 500-5000 microsiemens/cm (captures bulk dragout) |
| Intermediate rinse | City water, flowing | 100-500 microsiemens/cm |
| Final pre-plating rinse | DI water preferred | <50 microsiemens/cm (target) |
| Critical pre-plating rinse (electronics, decorative) | DI water required | <20 microsiemens/cm |

### Conductivity Targets by Application

| Application | Target Conductivity |
|---|---|
| General industrial plating | <200 microsiemens/cm |
| Decorative nickel/chrome | <100 microsiemens/cm |
| Electronics / connectors | <20 microsiemens/cm |
| Aerospace (per processor specification) | <50 microsiemens/cm |

### Neutralization Tank Chemistry

| Transition | Neutralization Solution | Concentration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkaline -> Acid | Dilute acid (1-5% H2SO4 or HCl) | 10-50 g/L acid | Remove alkaline residue; prevent alkaline dragout into acid bath |
| Acid -> Alkaline | Dilute alkali (1-3% NaOH or Na2CO3) | 10-30 g/L | Remove acid residue; prevent acid dragout into alkaline bath |
| Acid -> Plating (acidic bath) | Often no neutralization needed — direct rinse | N/A | Mild acid on surface is compatible with acid plating baths |
| Acid -> Plating (alkaline bath) | Dilute alkali or just thorough rinsing | Case-dependent | Prevent acid dragout into alkaline plating bath |

### Control Methods
- **Rinse water:** Continuous conductivity monitoring (inline probe); automatic dump or flow valve triggered at conductivity setpoint
- **Neutralization tanks:** pH monitoring; maintain pH 2-3 (acid neutralization) or pH 9-11 (alkaline neutralization) depending on purpose
- **DI water quality:** Monitor resistivity at DI unit output; typical target: 1-18 megohm-cm (conductivity 0.055-1.0 microsiemens/cm)

---

## 8.4 — Rinse Design / Treatment Stage Poster Data

### Rinse Types

| Rinse Type | Description | Effectiveness | Water Usage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Single immersion (stagnant)** | Parts dipped in standing water; no overflow | Poor — concentration builds rapidly | Lowest |
| **Single flowing** | Parts dipped in tank with continuous overflow | Moderate | Moderate |
| **Double counterflow (cascade)** | Two tanks; fresh water enters final tank, overflows into first tank | Excellent — each stage achieves ~10:1 dilution | Efficient |
| **Triple counterflow** | Three tanks; same cascade principle | Superior — each stage ~10:1; total ~1000:1 | Most efficient |
| **Spray rinse** | Water sprayed onto parts over a tank | Effective; very low water usage | Lowest flowing |
| **Drag-out rinse** | Stagnant first rinse dedicated to capturing concentrated dragout | Extends chemical bath life; recycles dragout | N/A (recirculated) |

### Rinse Ratio Concept
- **Rinse ratio (R)** = Volume of rinse water / Volume of dragout
- For a single flowing rinse to achieve 1000:1 dilution, R must be 1000
- For a **double counterflow** cascade to achieve 1000:1, each stage only needs R = ~31.6 (square root of 1000)
- **This is why counterflow cascade rinsing is standard** — it achieves high dilution ratios with dramatically less water

### Dragout Reduction Methods

| Method | Dragout Reduction |
|---|---|
| Slow withdrawal (5-10 sec drain) | 30-50% reduction |
| Drain rails/bars above tank | 20-40% reduction |
| Air knife (compressed air blow-off) | 50-70% reduction |
| Fog/mist spray above process tank | 40-60% reduction |
| Drag-out rinse (stagnant recovery) | Recovers 50-80% of dragout for return to process |
| Heated rinse (reduces viscosity, improves draining) | 10-20% reduction |

### Common Rinse System Failures

| Failure | Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Plating contamination/skip plating | Inadequate rinsing; conductivity too high | Measure conductivity; increase flow or add rinse stage |
| Chemical consumption spike in plating bath | Dragout carryover of incompatible chemistry | Check rinse water quality; add drag-out rinse |
| Staining between process steps | Parts drying in rinse (water spots); contaminated rinse water | Keep parts wet; check rinse water cleanliness |
| pH drift in plating bath | Acid or alkali dragout from upstream | Verify neutralization step is working; check rinse conductivity |

---

## 8.5 — DI Water System Poster Data

### DI vs. City Water

| Parameter | City Water | DI Water |
|---|---|---|
| Conductivity | 200-1000 microsiemens/cm (typical municipal) | 0.055-1.0 microsiemens/cm |
| Hardness | 50-500 ppm as CaCO3 | <1 ppm |
| Chloride | 10-250 ppm | <0.1 ppm |
| Silica | 5-50 ppm | <0.01 ppm |

### Why DI Water Matters for Plating
- Calcium and magnesium (hardness) form insoluble soaps in alkaline baths and interfere with plating
- Chloride contaminates plating baths and causes pitting
- Silica can cause haze and adhesion failure
- **Rule of thumb:** Any rinse that feeds directly into a plating tank should use DI water

### DI System Types
- **Mixed-bed ion exchange:** Standard; produces 1-18 megohm-cm water; regenerated with acid and caustic
- **Reverse osmosis (RO):** Removes 90-99% of dissolved solids; often used as pre-treatment before ion exchange
- **RO + mixed-bed:** Best combination; extends resin life while achieving highest purity

---

## 8.6 — Secondary Treatment / Neutralization Poster Data

### Acid Neutralization After Alkaline Cleaning
- Purpose: remove alkaline film from part surface before acid activation
- **Most common:** Simple immersion in dilute sulfuric acid (1-5% v/v, 10-50 g/L) at room temperature for 15-30 seconds
- Alternative: dilute HCl (1-5%) — acceptable but introduces chlorides
- Some shops skip dedicated neutralization and rely on the acid activation/pickle step itself

### Alkaline Neutralization After Acid Pickle
- Less common than acid neutralization
- Used when transitioning from acid pickle to an alkaline plating bath (e.g., alkaline zinc)
- Simple dilute NaOH or Na2CO3 dip (1-3%, 10-30 g/L) for 15-30 seconds
- Or rely on thorough rinsing

### Inhibited Acid Dip
- Some shops use an **inhibited acid dip** as the neutralization/activation step:
  - Dilute H2SO4 or HCl (3-10%) with a wetting agent and/or inhibitor
  - Removes alkaline films, activates the surface, and minimizes base metal attack
  - Immersion time: 15-60 seconds

### Passivation Rinse for Stainless After Pickle
- After pickling stainless steel, a passivation rinse or passivation tank is the neutralization equivalent
- See Cluster 4, Section 4.6 for full passivation details
- Key point: the passivation step both neutralizes residual acid AND restores the chromium oxide passive film

---

## 8.7 — Inspection & Handling Poster Data

### Water Break Test
- The water break test is performed **after the final cleaning rinse**, before entering the plating tank
- This is the definitive cleanliness gate for the entire pre-treatment sequence
- Per ASTM F22: water must sheet uniformly for 30 seconds minimum

### Rinse Water Monitoring
- **Conductivity meter** (inline or handheld) is the primary tool
- **pH strips or meter** to verify neutralization effectiveness
- **Visual:** Clear rinse water with no foam, cloudiness, or oil sheen

### Handling Between Rinses
- Parts must remain wet at all times between process steps
- **Flash rusting** begins within 2-5 minutes on exposed steel
- Transfer time between tanks should not exceed 30-60 seconds

### Common Defects from Rinse/Neutralization Failure

| Defect | Rinse/Neutralization Cause |
|---|---|
| Blistering | Alkaline residue trapped under plating |
| Skip plating | Acid residue or alkaline residue preventing activation |
| Staining / discoloration | Water spots from drying; contaminated rinse water |
| Rough deposit | Particulate dragout from upstream process |
| Pitting | Chloride contamination from city water or HCl carryover |
| Poor adhesion | Inadequate surface activation due to pH film on surface |

## 8.8 — Regulatory Poster Data

### Wastewater
- Rinse water is the **primary wastewater stream** from a plating shop
- Regulated under EPA 40 CFR Part 433 — Metal Finishing Point Source Category
- Must meet pretreatment standards before discharge to POTW

### Key Discharge Limits (40 CFR 433 — Daily Maximum for Existing Sources)

| Parameter | Daily Maximum |
|---|---|
| Cadmium | 0.69 mg/L |
| Chromium (total) | 2.77 mg/L |
| Copper | 3.38 mg/L |
| Lead | 0.69 mg/L |
| Nickel | 3.98 mg/L |
| Silver | 0.43 mg/L |
| Zinc | 2.61 mg/L |
| Cyanide (total) | 1.20 mg/L |
| Total Toxic Organics (TTO) | 2.13 mg/L |
| pH | 6.0-9.0 (continuous) |

**Note:** Local POTW limits may be more stringent than federal limits. Always check local industrial user permit.

### Cyanide Segregation — Life Safety
- **Cyanide-bearing rinse water MUST be segregated from acid rinse water at all times**
- Mixing acid + cyanide produces HCN gas: CN- + H+ -> HCN (gas)
- HCN is lethal at approximately 300 ppm in air
- EPA requires cyanide waste to be treated (alkaline chlorination or other approved method) before combining with acid waste streams
- Plating shops with both cyanide and acid processes must have **separate drain systems**

### F006 Sludge
- Wastewater treatment sludge from plating rinse water treatment is **F006 listed hazardous waste** per 40 CFR 261.31
- This applies regardless of the sludge's chemical composition
- F006 is one of the most common listed hazardous wastes in the metal finishing industry

### Hydrogen Gas — Explosion Risk Summary
- Electrocleaning, acid pickling, and acid activation all generate hydrogen gas
- Hydrogen LEL: 4% in air; UEL: 75% in air
- Autoignition temperature: 1000 deg F (538 deg C) — but static discharge can ignite hydrogen
- Ventilation must maintain hydrogen below 25% of LEL (1% in air) per NFPA
- Applicable: NFPA 70 (NEC), NFPA 497, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.106
