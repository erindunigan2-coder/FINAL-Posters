## Why This Poster Matters

Zinc plating alone provides sacrificial corrosion protection, but bare zinc corrodes to white zinc oxide ("white rust") within hours or days of exposure. The **passivation** step -- a chromate or trivalent conversion coating applied after plating -- dramatically extends corrosion life by forming a barrier film on the zinc surface.

This is the step most operators rush through ("just dip it and go"), but it is the step that determines whether parts pass or fail salt spray testing. Every zinc plating shop needs this poster.

---

## The Core Concept

**Passivation** (in the context of zinc plating) = a chemical conversion coating applied by immersing freshly plated zinc parts in a chromium-containing solution. The solution reacts with the zinc surface to form a thin, adherent, corrosion-resistant film of chromium-zinc oxide/hydroxide compounds.

This film is **not an electroplated deposit** -- it forms by chemical reaction, not by electrical current. The zinc surface is partially dissolved and converted into the protective film.

---

## The Post-Plating Sequence

The complete process after the zinc plating tank:

```
ZINC PLATE --> Drag-out Rinse --> Rinse --> BRIGHT DIP (optional) --> Rinse --> PASSIVATE --> Rinse --> DRY --> (Optional: SEALANT / TOPCOAT) --> Inspect
```

### Step 1: Drag-Out Rinse

Removes plating solution drag-out. Reduces contamination of subsequent rinses and the passivation bath.

### Step 2: Rinse

Clean water rinse. Must be thorough -- plating solution drag-in is the leading cause of passivation bath contamination (especially zinc metal buildup).

### Step 3: Bright Dip (Optional)

A brief immersion (5--30 seconds) in dilute nitric acid (0.5--2% v/v) or dilute hydrochloric acid:
- Removes any surface oxide or haze formed during rinsing
- Activates the zinc surface for uniform passivation
- Nitric acid preferred for general use -- polishes the zinc surface
- HCl preferred when organic contamination is present

### Step 4: Rinse

Brief rinse after bright dip. Prevents acid drag-in to the passivation bath (acid contamination shifts pH and degrades the chromate film).

### Step 5: Passivation (Chromate Conversion)

The core step. See detailed breakdown below.

### Step 6: Rinse

Gentle rinse after passivation. **Critical**: aggressive rinsing or hot water can damage the freshly formed film. Use cold to warm water (not hot), minimal agitation, brief immersion.

### Step 7: Dry

Air dry or low-temperature oven:
- Trivalent passivations: below 150 deg F (65 deg C)
- Hexavalent passivations: below 300 deg F (150 deg C)

Excessive heat damages the conversion coating and reduces corrosion resistance.

### Step 8: Sealant / Topcoat (Optional)

Some specifications require a secondary sealant (lacquer, wax, silicate sealer, or organic topcoat) over the passivation for additional corrosion protection. Common in automotive and aerospace applications. Modern trivalent systems increasingly rely on proprietary topcoats to match hexavalent performance.

## Passivation Types -- The Color System

The "color" of a passivated zinc part is determined by the passivation chemistry, and each color corresponds to a different level of corrosion protection.

### Trivalent Chromium Passivations (Cr3+ -- RoHS/REACH compliant)

| Color | Film Thickness | Salt Spray (White Rust) | Salt Spray (Red Rust) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Clear / Blue** | Thinnest | 24--72 hrs | 72--200 hrs | Most common; cosmetically bright; lowest protection of trivalent options |
| **Yellow (Iridescent)** | Medium | 72--120 hrs | 200--400 hrs | Mimics hex yellow appearance; proprietary formulations |
| **Black** | Medium-Thick | 72--120 hrs | 200--400 hrs | Decorative + functional; two-component systems common |
| **Thick Film / High-Performance** | Thickest trivalent | 120--200+ hrs | 400--720+ hrs | Latest-generation formulations; with topcoat can match hex yellow |

### Hexavalent Chromium Passivations (Cr6+ -- restricted by RoHS/REACH/ELV)

| Color | Film Thickness | Salt Spray (White Rust) | Salt Spray (Red Rust) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Clear / Blue** | Thinnest | 12--24 hrs | 72--150 hrs | Being phased out in favor of trivalent |
| **Yellow / Iridescent** | Medium | 96--200 hrs | 200--500 hrs | Classic "yellow zinc" -- the industry workhorse for decades |
| **Olive Drab (OD)** | Thickest hex | 200--500 hrs | 500--1000+ hrs | Military/defense; MIL-DTL-5541; heaviest hex film |
| **Black** | Medium-Thick | 72--120 hrs | 200--400 hrs | Decorative/functional |

**Key trend**: Industry is moving from hexavalent to trivalent due to RoHS, REACH, and ELV directives. Trivalent passivations are now standard for automotive, electronics, and general commercial work. Hexavalent persists in military/aerospace where specifications have not yet been updated.

## Passivation Operating Parameters

### Trivalent Passivation (General)

| Parameter | Range |
|---|---|
| Chemistry type | Cr3+ sulfate or Cr3+ chloride based |
| pH | 1.5--2.5 (typical); up to 3.0 for thicker films |
| Temperature | 70--140 deg F (21--60 deg C); elevated temp for thicker films |
| Immersion time | 30--90 seconds (clear/blue); 60--180 seconds (yellow/black/thick film) |
| Agitation | Gentle; avoid excessive turbulence that disturbs film formation |

**pH and film thickness**: Raising bath pH toward 2.5 allows thicker chromate film build -- the part can remain in the bath longer without zinc degradation occurring.

### Hexavalent Passivation (General)

| Parameter | Range |
|---|---|
| Chemistry type | CrO3-based (sodium dichromate, chromic acid) |
| pH | 1.0--2.0 (clear); 0.5--1.5 (yellow); variable for OD |
| Temperature | 70--100 deg F (21--38 deg C); room temperature typical |
| Immersion time | 5--30 seconds (clear); 15--60 seconds (yellow); 30--120 seconds (OD) |
| Agitation | Gentle rack movement |

### Self-Healing Property (Hexavalent Only)

Hexavalent passivation films contain soluble Cr6+ compounds within the film matrix. When the film is scratched or damaged, the soluble Cr6+ migrates to the damaged area and re-passivates the exposed zinc. This "self-healing" property is unique to hexavalent systems -- **trivalent passivations do not self-heal**. This is the primary technical argument for retaining hexavalent passivations in critical corrosion applications, and the reason military/aerospace specifications have been slow to transition.

## Passivation Bath Contamination -- What Kills the Bath

| Contaminant | Source | Threshold | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iron (Fe3+)** | Steel racks, parts, tank | >100--150 ppm | Yellow/brown discoloration; depletes Cr6+ by reducing it to Cr3+ |
| **Zinc (Zn2+)** | Drag-in from plating bath | No formal limit; high levels problematic | Film discoloration; interference with chromate formation |
| **Copper (Cu2+)** | Drag-in; rack contamination | >30 ppm | Co-deposits; darkens film; reduces corrosion performance |
| **Organics** | Brightener drag-in from zinc bath | Variable | Interferes with film formation; spotty or patchy passivation |
| **Chloride** | Drag-in from acid zinc KCl bath | Variable | Can accelerate zinc attack during passivation |

**The iron rule**: Iron contamination above 100--150 ppm in a chromate bath produces yellow/brown discoloration and cannot be practically removed. The bath must be dumped and rebuilt. Prevention (clean racks, thorough rinsing, no dropped steel parts) is the only viable strategy.

## Zinc Corrosion Mechanism -- Why Passivation Works

1. **Bare zinc** exposed to atmosphere --> zinc reacts with moisture and CO2 --> forms white zinc oxide/hydroxide/carbonate ("white rust") --> visible within hours to days
2. **Passivated zinc** --> chromate conversion coating acts as a barrier --> slows oxygen and moisture access to the zinc surface --> delays onset of white rust by 10--100x depending on film type
3. **After white rust** --> zinc is consumed --> base metal (steel) exposed --> iron oxide ("red rust") begins --> part has failed

**Sacrificial protection**: Zinc is anodic to steel (zinc corrodes preferentially to protect steel). Even after the passivation film is breached, the remaining zinc layer continues to protect the steel substrate by sacrificial galvanic action. The passivation extends the life of the zinc layer itself.

## Trivalent vs. Hexavalent -- Side-by-Side

| Feature | Trivalent (Cr3+) | Hexavalent (Cr6+) |
|---|---|---|
| **RoHS/REACH compliance** | Yes | No -- restricted substance |
| **Self-healing** | No -- film is inert once formed | Yes -- soluble Cr6+ migrates to scratches |
| **Corrosion resistance** | Good to excellent (with topcoat) | Excellent (especially yellow and OD) |
| **Colors available** | Clear, yellow, black, thick film | Clear, yellow, black, olive drab |
| **Drying temperature limit** | 150 deg F (65 deg C) | 300 deg F (150 deg C) |
| **Topcoat requirement** | Often required for best performance | Not required (self-healing compensates) |
| **Environmental/health risk** | Low | High -- Cr6+ is carcinogenic |
| **Industry trend** | Growing -- now standard for most work | Declining -- restricted to military/aerospace holdouts |

## Key Data Points for Callouts

**Salt spray performance (trivalent clear vs. hex yellow)**:
- Trivalent clear: `24--72 hrs` to white rust
- Hex yellow: `96--200 hrs` to white rust
- Thick-film trivalent with topcoat: `120--200+ hrs` -- approaching hex yellow

**Self-healing**:
- Hexavalent: `Yes` -- soluble Cr6+ migrates to damaged areas
- Trivalent: `No` -- film is inert once formed

**Passivation pH**:
- Higher pH --> thicker film --> more protection
- Typical range: `1.5--2.5`

**Iron contamination threshold**:
- `>100--150 ppm Fe3+` --> yellow discoloration, bath must be dumped

**Drying temperature limits**:
- Trivalent: `< 150 deg F (65 deg C)`
- Hexavalent: `< 300 deg F (150 deg C)`

**Regulatory**:
- RoHS, REACH, ELV directives restrict Cr6+
- Industry transition: hexavalent --> trivalent is ongoing globally

## Poster-Worthy Sticky Facts

1. **"The passivation makes or breaks the salt spray test"** -- most people think the zinc provides all the corrosion protection. In reality, the chromate conversion coating can multiply corrosion resistance by 10--100x. A clear trivalent passivation turns 2--4 hours of bare zinc survival into 24--72 hours. The passivation step is not optional.

2. **"Clear to OD: 24 to 500 hours"** -- the range of salt spray performance across passivation types spans a factor of 20x. Clear/blue trivalent at the bottom (24--72 hours to white rust), olive drab hexavalent at the top (200--500 hours). Color is not cosmetic -- it is corrosion performance made visible.

3. **"Self-healing: hex's secret weapon"** -- hexavalent passivation films contain soluble Cr6+ that migrates to scratches and re-passivates damaged areas. Trivalent films cannot do this. This single property is why military specifications have been slow to transition away from hexavalent chemistry.

4. **"Iron above 150 ppm = dump the bath"** -- iron contamination in a passivation bath cannot be practically removed. Once iron exceeds 100--150 ppm, the bath produces yellow-brown discolored parts and must be dumped and rebuilt. Prevention (clean racks, thorough rinsing) is the only strategy.

5. **"Don't rinse hot after passivation"** -- aggressive rinsing or hot water after passivation damages the freshly formed film. Trivalent films are particularly fragile -- never exceed 150 deg F during drying. Hexavalent films tolerate up to 300 deg F. The drying step is where corrosion performance is won or lost.

6. **"Drag-in is the #1 killer"** -- plating solution carried on parts into the passivation bath is the primary source of contamination (zinc, organics, chloride). Two seconds of extra rinsing prevents hours of troubleshooting and potential bath dumps.

7. **"Trivalent is the future"** -- RoHS, REACH, and ELV directives restrict hexavalent chromium globally. Modern thick-film trivalent systems with proprietary topcoats now approach hexavalent yellow performance. The transition is not a compromise -- it is an upgrade for most applications.
