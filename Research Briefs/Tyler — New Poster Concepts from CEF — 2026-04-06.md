## Context

The existing Plating Posters series (15 posters) covers the electroplating process, zinc plating, Hull Cell reading, anode-to-cathode ratio, passivation, metallic contamination, Faraday's Law, anodizing, electroless nickel, current density, pH control, barrel vs. rack, safety, surface preparation, and the quality loop.

The following five concepts fill gaps in the series -- all are hands-on, practical, shop-floor topics drawn from general industry knowledge in filtration, deposit testing, precious metals handling, part fabrication challenges, and hydrogen embrittlement. Each would be valuable as a wall reference in a working plating facility.

---

## Poster Concept 16: Filtration and Purification -- Keeping Your Bath Clean

The plating bath is only as good as its filtration system, yet filtration is the most neglected maintenance item in most shops. This poster would illustrate the three types of contamination that destroy plating quality -- particulate solids, dissolved metallic ions, and organic contaminants -- and show which removal method works for each. The centerpiece would be a decision tree: roughness points to filtration (cartridge type, porosity selection by process, turnover rate targets); dullness and HCD burning point to activated carbon treatment (adsorption mechanism, carbon type selection, batch treatment procedure); dark deposits and discoloration point to dummy plating or chemical precipitation for metals. Key technical points worth illustrating include the 4-5 turnover rule for 97% particulate removal, the 30-psi rat-holing threshold, the incompatibility of cellulose media with sulfuric acid, and the difference between adsorption (van der Waals, carbon) and absorption. A filtration media compatibility chart (polypropylene, polyimide, cellulose, stainless mesh) organized by solution type would be an outstanding quick-reference element. This poster would live near the filter station or maintenance board and get referenced every time a roughness or dullness complaint comes in.

## Poster Concept 17: Testing Your Deposit -- Thickness, Adhesion, and Hardness Methods

Every plated part must meet a specification, and the plater's ability to verify compliance depends on understanding the available test methods and their limitations. This poster would organize the most common deposit evaluation tests into three columns: Thickness (weigh-strip-weigh, coulometric stripping per ASTM B504, XRF per ASTM B568, eddy current per ASTM B244, magnetic gage, and cross-section microscopy), Adhesion (bend test per ASTM B489, heat-blister test per ASTM B571, tape-pull test, and the qualitative knife/chisel test), and Hardness (Knoop per ASTM E384/B578 and Vickers per ASTM E384, with a visual explanation of the anvil effect and minimum thickness requirements). Key numbers worth printing on the poster: the thickness unit conversion ladder (1 mil = 25.4 microns = 1000 micro-inches), heat-blister test temperatures by substrate (150C for zinc die castings, 250C for copper, 350C for steel), and the Knoop minimum deposit thickness for testing (38 microns for soft coatings, 25 microns for hard). This poster belongs on the QC lab wall, directly above the testing bench, and would be referenced daily by any shop that runs thickness or adhesion checks.

## Poster Concept 18: Precious Metals Plating -- Gold and Silver Quick Reference

Gold and silver plating have unique operating requirements that general-process platers rarely encounter, yet shops that handle these metals need concise, reliable reference data. This poster would present a side-by-side comparison of the major gold bath types (alkaline cyanide, neutral cyanide, acid cyanide, sulfite) and silver bath types (cyanide strike, normal Rochelle, high-speed), organized by pH range, metal concentration, current density, cathode efficiency, and primary application. Critical technical points to illustrate: the mandatory silver strike before plating over copper or nickel (immersion deposition mechanism), the dramatic efficiency difference between soft gold (98-100%) and hard gold (25-35%), the conductivity penalty of antimony-based silver brighteners (10-25% of pure silver), troy vs. avoirdupois unit differences (31.1 g vs. 28.35 g), and the gold sulfite stability limitation (pH must not drop below 8.0). The poster would serve two audiences: the precious metals plating technician who needs quick parameter verification, and the process engineer quoting or setting up a new precious metals job. It belongs in the lab or engineering office of any shop running gold or silver.

## Poster Concept 19: Hydrogen Embrittlement -- The Invisible Threat

Hydrogen embrittlement is the most dangerous failure mode in electroplating because it is completely invisible -- parts pass every visual and dimensional inspection and then fail catastrophically under load in service. This poster would explain the mechanism in plain terms: hydrogen generated at the cathode during plating diffuses into high-strength steel, concentrates at stress points and crack tips, and causes delayed brittle fracture. The key illustration would be a steel hardness scale showing the susceptibility threshold (Rc 35 / 200,000 psi tensile strength), the danger zone (Rc 36-43 where baking is mandatory), and the prohibition zone (above Rc 43 where certain processes are not allowed). Critical parameters to display prominently: bake temperature (375F +/- 25F), bake time (23 hours minimum for cadmium per ASTM B766, 3-24 hours depending on specification for other deposits), bake start window (within 1-4 hours of plating depending on specification), and the rule that chromate conversion must always follow baking, never precede it. The poster should also list which metals are susceptible (martensitic and PH stainless steels, high-strength carbon steels) and which are not (austenitic stainless, copper, aluminum). ASTM F519 (200-hour static load) and F1940 (step load, less than 24 hours) should be referenced as process verification tests. This poster belongs on the wall of any shop that plates high-strength steel -- aerospace fastener shops, military hardware, and automotive safety-critical parts.

## Poster Concept 20: Die Casting Defects and Their Impact on Plating

A large percentage of plating rejects on zinc and aluminum die castings trace back to the casting itself, not the plating process. This poster would illustrate the five most common die casting defects that cause plating failures: surface porosity (trapped air, insufficient fill), sub-surface porosity (chemicals migrate through the deposit by capillary action, causing blisters days or weeks later), cold shuts (discontinuous seams where metal solidified before the cavity filled), heat checks (thermal fatigue ridges on the die surface that transfer to every casting), and metal flow lines (visible under plate when gating is poorly designed). The central illustration would be a die casting cross-section showing the dense outer skin versus the porous interior, with a bold callout: "Over-polishing cuts through the dense skin and reveals the porous interior -- blistering is guaranteed." Key practical data points: die temperature tolerance (+/-5C), 5-6 shots between mold release reapplication, the heavy acid copper fill technique (0.001-0.002 inches followed by rebuffing) as a countermeasure for marginal porosity, and the prohibition on silicone-containing mold release compounds. This poster serves the decorative plating shop that receives die castings from external foundries -- it gives the plater the vocabulary and the visual evidence to push back on poor casting quality before wasting time and chemistry on parts that will never pass.
