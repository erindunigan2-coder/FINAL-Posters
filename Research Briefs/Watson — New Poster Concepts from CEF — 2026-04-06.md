# New Poster Concepts — 2026-04-06

Five new poster topics not in the existing 15-poster series, each grounded in industrial surface finishing knowledge. These topics fill gaps in the current library and address concepts plating shops encounter daily but rarely see visualized.

---

## Poster Concept 16: Rinsing Efficiency — The Hidden Cost of Poor Rinsing

**Process/Concept:** Counterflow rinsing, drag-out management, rinse ratio, and conductivity monitoring.

**Key Technical Points Worth Illustrating:** The counterflow rinse equation (FR = DI x (CT/CR)^(1/N)) is one of the highest-impact formulas a plating shop can implement, but most shops have never seen it written down. A visual comparison of single-rinse vs. 2-stage counterflow at a 100:1 rinse ratio — showing the 90% water reduction — would be immediately compelling. The poster should include: the counterflow flow direction (fresh water enters the cleanest tank, parts move dirtiest-to-cleanest), the 15-20 second drain time rule, drip tray slope direction, and a conductivity monitoring callout showing that inductive (toroidal) sensors resist fouling better than electrode sensors. Drag-out factors (part geometry, viscosity, surface tension) and drag-out recovery tanks round out the content.

**Why This Would Be Valuable as a Shop-Floor Poster:** Every plating shop uses water, and most use far more than necessary. Water and sewer costs are rising, and environmental discharge permits are getting tighter. This poster gives a shop supervisor the math and the layout to justify a counterflow rinse system to management — or to optimize an existing one. It belongs near the rinse stations or in the engineering office. No poster in the existing series covers rinsing, and it pairs naturally with Poster #1 (Surface Preparation) as part of the complete process line.

## Poster Concept 17: The Galvanic Series — Why Metals Corrode in Contact

**Process/Concept:** Galvanic corrosion, the EMF series, the galvanic series (sea water), anodic vs. cathodic deposits, and the four components of a galvanic cell.

**Key Technical Points Worth Illustrating:** A large, clear galvanic series chart showing common metals and alloys ranked from most active (magnesium, zinc, aluminum) to most noble (gold, platinum) would be the hero element — this is the kind of reference data that engineers look up regularly but never have posted on the wall. The four galvanic cell components (anode, cathode, electronic path, ionic path) should be illustrated with a callout: "Eliminate any one to stop corrosion." A visual example of anodic protection (zinc on steel — zinc corrodes sacrificially) vs. cathodic failure (nickel on steel — steel corrodes at pores) would drive home the practical consequence. Include the key potential differences: Zn/Steel = 0.31V (mild, protective), Au/Ni = 1.93V (severe, destructive).

**Why This Would Be Valuable as a Shop-Floor Poster:** Corrosion is the reason plating exists. Every plating shop should understand why zinc protects steel, why nickel-chrome on steel needs to be pore-free, and why mixing dissimilar metals in assemblies causes field failures. This poster serves quality engineers, design engineers, and plating shop supervisors alike. It is the conceptual backbone behind Poster #6 (Passivation Sequence) and Poster #7 (Metallic Contamination) and would complete a three-poster corrosion education series.

## Poster Concept 18: Deposit Thickness Testing — Methods, Ranges, and When to Use Each

**Process/Concept:** Thickness measurement methods: XRF, coulometric, eddy current, magnetic gage, microscopic cross-section, dropping test, and weigh-strip-weigh.

**Key Technical Points Worth Illustrating:** A comparison grid showing each method's principle, applicable coating/substrate combinations, accuracy, whether it is destructive or non-destructive, and the ASTM standard number. Key data to include: XRF (ASTM B568, non-destructive, any metal-on-metal, ~1 minute per reading), coulometric (ASTM B504, semi-destructive, uses Faraday's Law), eddy current (ASTM B244, non-conductive on conductive only, 0.0001-0.002 inch range), magnetic gage (non-magnetic on steel, 10% accuracy above 10 micrometers), and cross-section microscopy (referee method, destructive, calibrates all other methods). The unit conversion table is critical reference data: 0.0002 inch = 2 tenths = 200 micro-inches = 5.1 microns; 1 micron = 39.37 micro-inches.

**Why This Would Be Valuable as a Shop-Floor Poster:** Thickness is the single most common measurement a plating shop makes. QC inspectors, lab techs, and auditors all need to understand which method is appropriate for which coating system. Most shops own XRF and/or coulometric equipment but do not fully understand the limitations of each. This poster would live in the QC area and get referenced every time a measurement question arises. It pairs with Poster #15 (Quality Loop) and Poster #8 (Faraday's Law).

## Poster Concept 19: Alloy Plating Fundamentals — Brass, Bronze, and Zinc Alloys

**Process/Concept:** Co-deposition of two metals from a single bath — the Nernst equation applied to alloy plating, complexing agents, and the major commercial alloy systems (brass Cu-Zn, bronze Cu-Sn, zinc-nickel, zinc-cobalt).

**Key Technical Points Worth Illustrating:** The central concept is that two metals can only co-deposit when their deposition potentials are within approximately 0.2V of each other. A visual showing copper's standard potential (+0.34V) being shifted to -1.17V by cyanide complexing — bringing it near zinc at -0.76V — is the "aha moment" that makes alloy plating make sense. The poster should include a comparison table of the four major alloy systems: brass (Cu-Zn, cyanide, color control by free CN), bronze (Cu-Sn, cyanide/stannate, chromium substitute as white bronze), zinc-nickel (12-15% Ni, cadmium replacement, 3-5x salt spray vs. pure zinc), and zinc-cobalt (0.5-1% Co, budget alternative to Zn-Ni). Include the alloy Faraday constant formula: F_alloy = 100 / [(W1/F1) + (W2/F2)].

**Why This Would Be Valuable as a Shop-Floor Poster:** Alloy plating is growing — zinc-nickel for automotive and aerospace, brass for decorative work, bronze as a chrome substitute. Most platers understand single-metal deposition but find alloy co-deposition mysterious. This poster demystifies the electrochemistry in a visual way and provides the practical reference data (composition targets, applications) that process engineers need. It fills a gap between Poster #2 (The Electroplating Process) and Poster #8 (Faraday's Law).

## Poster Concept 20: Corrosion Testing at a Glance — Salt Spray, CASS, Humidity, and Beyond

**Process/Concept:** Accelerated corrosion test methods used to evaluate plated coatings: ASTM B117 (neutral salt spray), ASTM B368 (CASS), ASTM B287 (acetic acid salt spray), ASTM B380 (Corrodkote), ASTM D2247 (humidity), and ASTM B605 (SO2).

**Key Technical Points Worth Illustrating:** A comparison table of the six major test methods showing: ASTM number, solution/environment, pH, typical duration, what it simulates, and which coating systems it is specified for. The hero callout should be: "Salt spray testing is a relative comparison tool, NOT a predictor of service life." Include the solutions — B117 uses 5% NaCl at pH 6.5-7.2; CASS adds 0.25 g/L CuCl2 and drops to pH 3.1-3.3; Corrodkote uses a slurry of salts and kaolin clay. A visual salt spray cabinet cross-section showing fog distribution, panel angle (15-30 degrees from vertical), and collection cup placement would make the test accessible to operators who run it but do not understand it.

**Why This Would Be Valuable as a Shop-Floor Poster:** Nearly every plating spec references salt spray hours. Quality inspectors run these tests daily but often do not understand what the test is actually measuring or what its limitations are. The misconception that "more salt spray hours = longer field life" is pervasive and costly. This poster educates the entire team — from operators to quality managers — and provides the ASTM reference numbers that auditors will ask for. It pairs with Poster #15 (Quality Loop) and the new Poster #17 (Galvanic Series) for a complete corrosion science education set.
